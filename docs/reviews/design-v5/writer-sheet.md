## What this is

The technical design (`docs\design\`, tag `design-v4`) says how the system works inside. The experience design (`docs\experience\`, tag `experience-v1`) says what each person sees, receives and decides. While designing the experience we found 29 things the system must do that the technical design does not say yet. They are listed, with the reason for each and the design section where it belongs, in `docs\experience\15-asks-of-the-technical-design.md` (29 rows in four tables; read it first and read it whole).

Your job: put the rows that belong in YOUR file into that file, so a builder who reads only the technical design builds what the experience promises. The result is the new edition `design-v5`. The experience documents are settled: do not change them and do not argue with them, except where a statement is factually wrong against Microsoft Learn or contradicts the technical design in a way you cannot resolve (then report it, see "Report").

## Settled wording (shared by every file)

S1. **Plan code.** The Approval record binds the plan hash, the approver, the expiry and consumption (this exists). The short code a person sees (eight characters from the plan hash) is a display reference, not a secret and not a token. Each approver records one decision against the plan; a second submit by the same person is refused; the other approver's decision still counts; the plan is consumed once, when the Runner runs it. Never write that an approval or a code "works once" or is "single-use": the plan runs once.
S2. **One-tap cap.** R1 approvals are capped per approver per day: a starting value of five. After the cap, further R1 changes go to the approval page (the R2 route); nothing is blocked and nobody is alerted. Reaching the cap, or approving outside stated hours, is not an alarm and is never counted against anyone.
S3. **The one automatic approval alarm.** An approval recorded without the authenticated event of its own route (for a card: a delivery and a tap; for the approval page: a sign-in and a submit) raises an alarm and pauses that person's one-tap approvals until a person has looked. It catches forged requests; it does not prove who tapped and it does not catch a stolen signed-in session. A person's own report ("I did not approve this", on a card) starts the incident path whatever the record shows: it revokes the approval if it has not run, pauses that person's one-tap approvals, alerts the platform team and keeps the record. A report that a message looks wrong or fake ("Talk to a person") goes into the same path. Undo, not that report, reverses a change that ran.
S4. **Rollback.** Only an R1 change rolls back by itself when its check fails. For R2 and R3 a failed check stops, pauses the Credentials that cover the resource, and asks the approver, who restores in one tap inside the undo window: a rollback grant, usable once, held by the approver for a change that can be reversed. A purchase cannot be undone.
S5. **Three pauses, three owners.** "Pause messages" is a person's own notification setting (a return date; no non-urgent asks, reminders or routing to cover until then). "Pause changes here" is a flag an owner sets on resources in their scope: it stops Credential runs there at once, can only narrow what the system may do, leaves suggestions alone, and only a person resumes it. The kill switch belongs to the platform team and has three levels (everything; one kind of change in every estate; one estate). Undoing a change made under a Credential also pauses that Credential until a person has reviewed it.
S6. **Coverage, not escalation.** A waiting Case: one private reminder at day 7 (its own message, or inside the person's weekly summary once the person's push budget is used up); parked with an expiry at day 14; the owner is told once, first, by a notice e-mail, whenever cover is about to be asked (a parked item, or a real deadline that is close); cover (the delegate the owner appointed, else the custodian queue, the team named for unowned estate) is asked with a Question that says only "Ownership or cover needs confirming. Nothing is authorised."; a line manager is asked only if the owner appointed that manager as delegate; no message tells a third person that a named person did not answer. Routing authorises nothing: whoever takes the item sees the same plan and gives their own OK.
S7. **Ownership.** A directory candidate (a tag, a role assignment, the creator) is a guess. Until the person confirms responsibility, they receive only the first-contact message and Questions.
S8. **Conversation buttons.** Explain, Challenge and Teach on messages that carry a claim or a decision about spend; Explain alone on the rest (first contact, a question, settings, a confirmation, a housekeeping message); "Talk to a person" is the last button of every card, in the same place. A note (Lesson) never has a wider scope than its author owns; a wider scope is a request to the person who owns it, with the number of resources affected.
S9. **Send step.** Agents draft; workflows send. The send step: takes the recipient from the directory's owner of record, never from the draft; strips URLs and escapes markdown in every bound value; builds every link from a configured origin plus an opaque request id (a card carries one link, to the page of its request; a letter may carry several, a page per item); fills numbers, dates and money in model-written text from tool results at render time, in the person's locale. A model never supplies a recipient or a link.
S10. **Teams delivery.** One activity per message: a notification sentence in `text`, the card as the attachment, the AI-generated entity, a locale. Sends are queued per person and throttled per app and per conversation. The conversation reference is stored per person and retired on a 403 blocked-or-uninstalled response; the item then goes by e-mail. 429, 412, 502 and 504 are retried with back-off. A send whose result is unknown is recorded as unknown and checked before it is retried. Buttons are idempotent. The request id on every send is for matching, not a promise that a retry never sends twice. Cards go only to members of the directory, through the stored one-to-one conversation with the app; partners and other outsiders get e-mail only.
S11. **Verb contract.** A card button sends an opaque request id, a verb and, when it decides something, the plan code; at most an option key, a scope or a reason chip joins them. Only Challenge, Teach and the text boxes send words (bounded, screened, quoted as untrusted data); free text never approves. For each verb of each card the card manifest lists the inputs it accepts, whether it needs the plan code and what a tap returns; one contract covers every verb: who may tap (the card's addressee; for a decision, the designated approver or a named delegate, by directory object id), what a repeated tap does (nothing: the service answers "already recorded"), when a verb is refused (the request is expired, changed, answered or paused) and what a tap returns (one immediate response, then the outcome by updating the same message).
S12. **Pages.** The page of one request is `/ok/<requestId>`: for a decision it shows the exact plan from the stored plan record and takes the decision; for any other card it shows the card's own words and answers; opening it records no decision. Other routes: the notebook (the full reasoning of a message), a person's decisions, what the system knows about a person's resources (the Lessons), what the system did there (Credential runs, with Undo and "Pause changes here"), settings, and the kill switch (platform team only). Each page filters on the server by the signed-in person's scope.
S13. **External e-mail.** A request to a partner or a billing owner is plain-text e-mail with: an external flag, minimum estate data (only the scope identifiers needed to quote or buy: the management group or the subscriptions the purchase applies to; no resource names, owners, tags or other spend), no figures in the subject line, one recipient address that the platform team has put on the allow-list, no link and no button. A reply is read as a question and approves nothing.
S14. **AI marks.** Every message carries the notice that an AI wrote it, four ways: the sender name ends "(AI)"; a footer line; the AI-generated entity on a Teams message; and a machine-readable mark (the entity in Teams, the header `x-ai-generated: true` in e-mail), with a test that the marks survive delivery.
S15. **Atlas proposals come from a fork.** The `pr-draft` GitHub App holds Pull requests and Checks write and no Contents write on the base repository; it proposes from a fork. Merging needs Contents write, so a person merges.
S16. **Report data feed.** Small business events with no object id; counts over fewer than five people are rolled up and results that a subtraction could isolate are suppressed; the table keeps at least 13 months (a year-on-year commitment comparison needs 13); the Workbooks are kept as code. Owners and team leads see pages (filtered on the server), not Workbooks: a Workbook cannot filter per viewer beyond Azure roles.
S17. **Saving rule.** A saving claimed for a change excludes the unused commitment the change creates. A reduction made while a spending commitment is behind is held back from Verified, labelled "freed capacity inside the commitment", until the shortfall and its prepayment credit are settled. The commitment's pace (as of the last invoice date, with milestones and a shortfall range, or "Cannot assess") is a report field where the agreement makes it readable (direct agreements only).
S18. **Monthly letter.** Finance and executives get one letter a month with a small CSV, after the period closes (a starting value of five days after it ends, because usage charges can change until then); each figure says whether it is an estimate or final; an invoice status per billing scope: Reconciled, Unreconciled or Invoice not available.
S19. **Feedback capture.** Two signals go into the ledger: "useful" and "matches what I expected". They never change a Case.
S20. **First contact.** Before the system writes to anyone, the platform team or a team lead announces it in the organisation's own channels: the app's name, its publisher and the one address of the approval page; that it never asks for passwords or codes; that a sign-in asking to grant an app permissions is fraud. The person then installs the app or receives a first-contact e-mail; an opt-out is recorded and honoured.
S21. **Approver readiness.** Onboarding probes whether an approval page can ask for step-up (a Microsoft Entra authentication context) and whether every holder of an approver role has a phishing-resistant sign-in method registered. Where not: R3 purchases are refused with an explanation. Without authentication contexts only R1 works, in Teams; anything above one tap is refused with an explanation. Teams not installable: first contact by e-mail and every decision, even a one-tap one, on the approval page.
S22. **Kill switch page.** Three levels; a fresh sign-in each time (a borrowed or left-open session cannot stop the system); any one of the platform owner, the FinOps lead or the security reviewers can pull it; every pull and resume goes to the audit log and alerts the platform team and the security reviewer; resuming is staged and resuming purchases takes two people with the two roles of a two-person approval; a runbook gives the commands for the same switch if the page is down (and the failure drills rehearse it). It cannot recall a purchase already submitted.
S23. **Standing permissions (Credentials).** A Credential is written, reviewed and ended on a page by the FinOps lead and the platform owner together: a dry-run on history, the evidence, the expiry and the step-down. Before its first run, each owner of a resource it covers is told what it covers and until when, and how to pause it; each run is reported to that owner within minutes and added to their weekly summary. A Credential never covers a purchase, a trial or a resource an owner marked do-not-touch.
S24. **Templates.** Cards, letters, pages and message catalogs (per language) live in Git with CI checks: the card lint, a renderer check with Microsoft's Adaptive Cards libraries, snapshot tests and a comprehension check. A device test on every supported Teams client (the client matrix) and an accessibility audit gate a release that changes a template or a page.
S25. **Preferences.** One record per directory object id: channel, language, working days, time zone, quiet hours, desk day (the weekly summary), weekly push budget, delegates, mutes, and "Pause messages" with a return date. No names. A person can export or delete their preferences and their own notes; approval audit records are not erased (they hold only an object id) and follow the adopting organisation's retention.
S26. **Notification policy.** A step in the send workflow, in code: push accounting per person (an *ask* is a Question or a Recommendation; a *push* is any message the system starts unprompted); a slow start (one push a week for the first 30 days after a person's own first contact, then three; starting values; the FinOps lead sets the ceiling, a person may lower it); one reminder; coverage routing (S6); the emergency lane (opt-in per scope, absolute thresholds, an on-call route; voice or SMS only through the organisation's own paging); a weekly desk (a summary of at most five items); a fatigue review (counts for groups of at least five, never names). The Conductor cannot raise any of it.
S27. **Experience tests are not a proof of understanding.** Card lint, renderers, snapshots and the comprehension check show that the information is there; they do not show that a person understands it or decides well, and the renderings are not Teams' own.

## Amendments given to the writers while they worked

1. **S1, "single-use".** The phrase "single-use" may stay in design text where it means that the Runner consumes the approval record once, when it runs. It must never be said of the plan code, and nothing may imply that the second approver of a purchase is refused.
2. **S24, the gates.** The gates are those of the experience design (`docs\experience\12-testing-the-experience.md`, "The eight layers" and "Release gates"): on every commit and merge, the card lint, the renderer check and, for every template and language, the comprehension check; before a new card type, page or language goes live, people tests (five people read it cold, a ten-second test, a native-speaker review, assistive technology in Teams); before go-live of the Teams cards and the pages, the client matrix; every year and after a page redesign, the accessibility audit. The client matrix and the audit do not gate every release that changes a template or a page.
