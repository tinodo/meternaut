# Design 13 — Decisions

> **What this is:** the architecture decision records (ADRs) of design phase 1. Each decision names the **strongest alternative** and why it lost, what the decision **costs and locks in**, and what would make us **revisit** it. Every ADR was put to two rubber-duck rounds, each lens on a different model family from its lens in the other round ([review record](../reviews/README.md)). **Status** says what happened after the review: **Accepted** — two rounds, no substantive change in the second; **Accepted with changes** — the second round or the targeted third pass changed it and the change is applied; **Proposed** — the second round reversed or redefined it and the third pass, by two further models, still found defects in the redesign (all fixed in the text): reviewed three times and **unproven** until the spikes named in its *Revisit when* have run. A status is never "final": each record's *Revisit when* names the spike or the event that reopens it. Evidence is in the [research](research/) and the [feature register](research/feature-register.md); the requirements are in [01](01-requirements-and-principles.md). Part of the [design](README.md).

**How to read a record.** *Reversibility*: **easy** (a config or contract change), **moderate** (a redeploy or migration), **one-way** (cannot be changed without re-creating a resource). *Lock-in*: what we would have to replace to leave. *Preview*: whether the decision depends on a preview feature (and where its fallback is — [09 §5](09-safeguards-and-drift-controls.md#5-the-preview-register-and-the-dependency-register)).

## Decisions

### ADR-01 A deterministic spine calls agentic leaves
**Status** Accepted · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** a partner's agent must be called, or an A2A path through the hub is generally available and its authentication is unambiguous.
**Decision.** Workflows are deterministic and contain no model. They invoke **hosted agents** (fourteen roles, fifteen identities) one step at a time through the hub with an envelope of ids and budgets. Agents never call each other; they read and write typed records ([05 §1](05-agents-and-orchestration.md#1-the-pattern-a-deterministic-spine-with-agentic-leaves)). The spine may branch, fan out competing hypotheses and loop back to an earlier step; the evidence is an immutable, versioned record graph that keeps contrary evidence.
**Because.** It removes the *agent-to-agent* channel along which an injected instruction would travel (free text inside typed records is still a path, which is why the source sanitiser, the typed-field policy and the session taint rule exist), makes budgets and retries deterministic, and makes each leaf separately testable and replaceable.
**Strongest alternative.** Agents that hand off to each other (Agent Framework handoff, group chat, or A2A through the hub): more fluid collaboration and less schema work. Rejected because each hop is an injection path, A2A through the hub is preview with contradictory authentication notes, and cost control moves into prompts.
**Consequences.** Schema upkeep; less emergent behaviour; every leaf needs a typed output. No lock-in beyond the Agent Framework inside the leaves and Durable Functions for the spine ([ADR-33](#adr-33-the-spine-is-durable-functions-on-flex-consumption-calling-leaves-through-the-hub)).
**Round 1** (A-10, D-01): bounded branching and a record graph with contrary evidence added; the over-claim that nothing is left for an injection to travel along removed.

### ADR-02 The gateway is the Citadel Governance Hub on API Management
**Status** Accepted · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the AI Gateway product meets the criteria in [04 §9](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md#9-the-ai-gateway-product-a-decision-gate).
**Decision.** All model, tool and agent traffic passes the Citadel Governance Hub, forked and pinned to a commit, with contracts in our repository ([04](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md)). The fork is hardened (`useTargetAzureKeyVault` set to true and no key-bearing deployment outputs; `x-policy-id` and API-version overrides stripped), and **each agent is authorised per tool** by its verified `azp`/`oid` and app roles assigned directly to its identity; the `x-sub-agent-id` header is attribution only.
**Because.** It is the framework the brief names, it is hub-and-spoke, and its contracts (backend, access, publish) are exactly the supply, demand and publishing surfaces we need; API Management's AI policies are generally available.
**Strongest alternative.** The new standalone AI Gateway product (calendar budgets, tool-level governance) — preview, two regions, no price, policies that do not match Citadel's contracts. A second alternative, Foundry's portal gateway, can create a second API Management next to the hub, may be able to associate an existing one (tested in S-NET-1c), and routes only some tools.
**Consequences.** The hub is a **single-maintainer sample** with no releases; a fixed monthly floor of about €320 in its usage pipeline unless shared or trimmed; contracts as data keep exit cheap. Risk R4 in [08](08-governance-and-compliance.md#8-risk-register).
**Round 1** (B-03, E-05, E-06): per-tool authorisation by verified identity (spike S-GW-3), the hardening additions, and the portal-gateway claim softened.

### ADR-03 Two spokes, a Lab management group and read-only estates
**Status** Accepted · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the non-production floor of a standard-setup Foundry account proves too costly ([12](12-cost-and-finops.md)).
**Decision.** `acia-prod` and `acia-nonprod` spokes; vended Lab subscriptions in their own management group; estates are other subscriptions onboarded in two consented parts — **read** onboarding, and a separate **action** onboarding that grants the broker a constrained delegation ([04 §3](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md#3-topology), [ADR-07](#adr-07-execution-identities-are-granted-just-in-time-by-a-broker-on-a-signed-approval)).
**Because.** Environments need separate trust boundaries and separate quota; the Lab must have no route to anything real.
**Strongest alternative.** One subscription with resource groups per environment: cheaper, one quota pool, and a single noisy agent group can starve the others.
**Consequences.** Three or more subscriptions to vend; the Foundry standard setup repeats **per private account** (an AI Search service, a Cosmos DB account and a Storage account each, priced in [12](12-cost-and-finops.md)); accounts are created in the phase that needs them, and a cheaper shape for dev and production is spike **S-NET-2**.
**Round 1** (C-01, C-07, B-01): the floor is priced per account and creation is staged.

### ADR-04 Sweden Central is the primary region
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** one-way per Foundry account · **Revisit when** West Europe gains managed VNet, the full AI Landing Zone deployment and serverless search.
**Decision.** Sweden Central hosts the spokes; the hub is wherever the landing zone puts it ([04 §3.2](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md#32-region)).
**Because.** It is the only EU region present in every capability list that matters (hosted agents, managed VNet, the AI Landing Zone, the hub's region list); serverless search is also offered in Denmark East and Poland Central, and Learn flags Sweden Central for new search services (see the consequences).
**Strongest alternative.** West Europe: the likely home of the operator's landing zone, about 23 % cheaper API Management (Standard v2 is €0.84 a unit-hour there against €1.10 in Sweden Central), and hosted agents are listed there — but no hosted-agent meter appears in the price API, and managed VNet and the AI Landing Zone are absent.
**Consequences.** Cross-region peering if the connectivity hub differs; a different region for the Foundry account later means re-creating it. Learn flags Sweden Central as "in high demand, which prevents the creation of new search services", so the Search services are created on day zero, with another EU region as the fallback. Learn's pages disagree on whether the Search service may live in another region than the Foundry account (the networking page says only the Foundry resource and the VNet must share a region; the Agent Service FAQ says all resources must), so spike S-NET-3 decides; cross-region traffic is costed in [12](12-cost-and-finops.md).

### ADR-05 Bring-your-own-VNet injection, not the managed VNet
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** one-way · **Revisit when** managed VNet offers outbound logging, a bring-your-own firewall and West Europe.
**Decision.** Agents run in a delegated `/24` subnet of our spoke; egress passes the landing zone's firewall with an FQDN allow-list ([04 §6](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md#6-network)).
**Because.** The managed VNet's FQDN rules create a managed Azure Firewall — **only when an outbound FQDN rule is added** (with service-tag and private-endpoint rules alone there is none), Standard by default, Basic selectable and then fixed at deployment ([Learn, managed virtual network](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/foundry/how-to/managed-virtual-network#pricing)) — which bills **€803 a month for the Standard deployment hour plus €0.014 a GB** in Sweden Central; and the managed VNet has no outbound logging and no custom firewall. Agents that must reach the Microsoft Learn or GitHub FQDNs need FQDN rules, so the firewall is the likely case.
**Strongest alternative.** Managed VNet: no address planning and fewer resources for us to own.
**Consequences.** We own the subnet; subnet exhaustion is silent (HTTP 429), so it is monitored; the choice is made at account creation and cannot be changed. Learn also contradicts itself on whether West Europe supports the managed VNet (an explicit list without it, and "all regions that support bring-your-own-VNet"), so that "no" rests on one reading; S-NET-2 pins it.
**Round 2** (E-07): the firewall exists only with an FQDN rule; "cannot be switched off" removed; the region contradiction named.

### ADR-06 An Entra agent identity per agent and eleven contract groups
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the hub accepts identity without a shared key.
**Decision.** Each hosted agent has its own Entra agent identity, a named sponsor and no standing roles; agents are gathered into **eleven groups** (study, curate, survey, investigate, experiment, converse, engineer, govern, examine, forge, conduct) — fourteen roles and fifteen identities, because the Librarian runs twice — with **one hub contract per group, data class and environment**: `engineer` and `govern` are EU-only and `examine` has a synthetic and a real-estate contract. Authorisation is **per tool**, by the verified `azp`/`oid` and app roles assigned directly to the agent identity ([05 §2](05-agents-and-orchestration.md#2-the-roster), [04 §4](04-landing-zone-and-citadel.md#43-the-access-contracts)).
**Because.** The groups are the capability classes of the lethal-trifecta matrix; the Scholar (the one reader of untrusted web text) and the Librarian (the Atlas writer) are apart; the Forger and the Conductor are separate so the adversary shares nothing with the examinee and the budget holder cannot spend another group's budget; and no contract mixes data classes.
**Strongest alternative.** One shared project identity per Foundry project (the legacy behaviour) or three coarse groups: fewer contracts, blurred blast radius.
**Consequences.** Per-agent attribution and per-tool authorisation depend on the token's `azp` and roles for agent identities (spikes S-ID-2 and S-GW-3); until then a header carries attribution only and **authorisation fails closed at the group**: the hub refuses a call whose verified identity it cannot map to a listed agent, rather than treating a valid group key as enough. Conditional Access for agents is licence-bound and optional. Eleven groups across environments is many contracts: they are data, generated from one table, and a phase profile creates only the ones the phase needs.
**Round 1** (A-02, B-02, B-03): nine groups became eleven; `govern` no longer mixes data classes; the header is no longer an authorisation input.
**Round 2** (B-04, A-11): the group fallback fails closed; contracts are created by phase.
**Third pass** (T-B, TB-01 and TB-03; Agree with changes): `tools/cost-model.py --check` now guards each contract's pools and whether it has a PROD product, the phase profile of 14 §4 and the environments a scenario creates; at Pilot scale (no TEST account) DEV carries the exams and the gates, so its quotas are sized for them.
**Closure check** (T-B; **Agree**): TB-03's residual is closed by tool: `--check` now also compares the Agents column of 04 §4.3 with the roster of 05 §2, every number of roles, identities, groups and contracts that a document states with those tables, and the pipelines paragraph of 11 §3 with the phase profile of 14 §4, and `--self-test` holds a fixture for each.

### ADR-07 Execution identities are granted just in time by a broker on a signed approval
**Status** Proposed (redesigned in Round 2; the third pass and its closure check found further defects in the redesign and they are fixed in the text, but the mechanics stay unproven until spikes S-ID-1, S-ID-3 and S-ACT-1 have run, and nothing changes an estate before they pass) · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** PIM supports time-bound activation for workload identities with our licences.
**Decision.** *Several parties; no single credential completes a change that needs a person's web-page approval.* One order, and no party trusts another's say-so. The **spine** allocates a decision id and computes the target hash over it; the **Policy service** signs a **permit** that carries both. A person approves on the web page: the page offers **Approve** and **Decline**, and a click on Approve, and only that click, starts an Entra sign-in whose `nonce` binds the target hash, the decision *approve* and a challenge from the Brief, so a sign-in made for any other decision never verifies as an approval. The **Approval service** authenticates the approver, signs the approval record with a Key Vault key that only it can use, appends the approval's entry through the Audit Writer, creates a signed **`approval-position` marker** that holds the entry's ledger position (the record is never amended, and the service holds no ledger read) and **enqueues** the record id; **it starts nothing** and holds no `Microsoft.App/jobs/*` action. The **credential broker** — small and deterministic, holding *Role Based Access Control Administrator* limited by condition to our custom Move roles and the execution identities — verifies the Approval signature, **that the approval's own block is in the ledger (it reads the block; the Audit Writer's acknowledgement is not the proof)**, the Policy permit, the person's token (issuer, audience, the decision-bound nonce, freshness of `iat` and `auth_time` against the ledger's append time for the approval's entry, `amr`, approver standing read live), for two approvers both tokens (two different people, whose `oid`s it reads from the tokens, in two different hats read live and written into its signed grant-intent, neither the requester nor the preparer, excluded by a per-decision hash), the approval epoch and the deny lists, for an autonomy-credential basis the permit and target against **the credential's own signed record**, and the single-use nonce marker; it writes a signed **grant-intent** entry and reads it back, takes the **lease**, reads `controls` fresh for a deployment lock, creates the role assignment at the exact target scope, writes the outcome and enqueues the run on its Move class's `run` queue. The **Move runner** is an **event-driven job** with a template fixed by the pipeline (only the pipeline that deploys the environment — `deploy` for dev and test, `release` for production — may write it, the production pipeline holds no start right, and any other start is detection D16); it re-verifies everything independently (both tokens' `oid`s and the hats that the broker recorded included), reads the epoch, the deny lists and the stop flag fresh before every step, writes a **step-intent** entry, checks the live ARM state, acts, and writes its outcome and its done marker. The **Verifier** is an event-driven job that wakes on the `verify` queue, to which the spine enqueues an id when the runner reports `execution finished`. Execution identities are **leased exclusively** (one Move at a time); revocation is probed in two parts (the assignment list shows none, and a call the role would have allowed fails after the propagation wait) and the identity stays quarantined until both pass; an independent delete-only sweeper, which holds no key and writes nothing (the Verifier records what it removed, from the Activity Log), and a Resource Graph alert catch leftovers. Every item that widens authority in the control store carries the signature of the party that may widen it, and every marker is signed by its producer over its canonical JSON, location included, so that a signature cannot be moved to another item: container-level RBAC cannot separate item types, so integrity is an item-level rule ([07 §3.3](07-security-architecture.md#33-the-execution-identity-just-in-time-without-pim), [07 §4](07-security-architecture.md#4-approvals-that-cannot-be-forged-or-reused)).
**Because.** PIM cannot make managed identities or service principals eligible; standing write roles would violate Q-SEC-1; and *Jobs - Start* accepts an execution template that can override a job's image, command and environment, so **whoever may start the runner can run any image with its identities** ([Learn, jobs permissions](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/container-apps/jobs#permissions)). The signer must therefore not hold it, and the broker and the runner must not trust the signer alone.
**Strongest alternative.** PIM time-bound *active* assignments for the execution identities (unverified for service principals); standing roles guarded only by the approval check; or the Round-1 design in which the Approval service dispatched the runner from a fixed template — rejected in Round 2, because the start call can override the template and cannot carry an approval id.
**Consequences.** The delegation can constrain roles, principal ids and principal type but **not the target scope**, so target scoping is deterministic code plus detection, not ABAC; the estate must consent to a separate *action* onboarding. RBAC changes can take up to ten minutes to propagate, so purchase windows and the kill-switch table say so. **The residual, stated plainly** (the canonical wording is in [07 §3.3](07-security-architecture.md#33-the-execution-identity-just-in-time-without-pim)): *no single credential completes a change that needs a person's web-page approval.* A compromised Approval service cannot start a job, fabricate a permit or fabricate a person's Entra-signed token. On the **card path** (reversible Moves of the lowest class only) the proof is the bot channel's assertion, which a compromised Approval service can fabricate; that is why the path ends at that class and D13 cross-checks it. A compromised Approval **and** Policy service can approve within an **autonomy credential's** constraints, which the broker and the runner check against the credential's own signed record, not against the permit. A compromised **broker** can grant the constrained Move roles to the execution identities at any opted-in scope, whether or not one is leased, because the delegation cannot tell; a leftover is usable only by that class's runner job, which wakes for any message in its queue, believes nothing in it and verifies everything itself (a queue is a doorbell, and its consumer can also put to it, because renewing a message needs `messages/write`), and D15 flags a grant that matches no signed approval. A compromised **release pipeline**, the only holder of `jobs/write`, can run code as a runner identity while that identity holds a role; that is bounded by the lease (one target, one class, minutes), by signed-image admission and by the two-approver release rule, and detected by D16. A compromised **Verifier** can sign a false success, so a saving counts in the headline only when the Auditor has **corroborated** it from the cost export through its own read path (every purchase and every Move above the materiality threshold; the rest stays *claimed*); the sweeper writes nothing, and a scheduled observer job of the Verifier records what it removed. The approval page is the person's display: a compromised page can show one transaction and submit another, or start the approve sign-in without a click (the nonce is chosen by the app, so it proves a sign-in for that decision, not a click), which the short code in two channels, the two-approver rule, D13 and the card limit bound but do not remove. Each party is small, typed (C#), property-tested, has no model inside and is separately monitored. Spikes S-ID-1, S-ID-3 (key pins; the click-bound sign-in and its nonce; the claims; forged, copied and squatted markers; the deployment-lock races and the epoch race under Strong consistency and, as a control, under Session; signed wait counters; the observer; kills at four points; the standing watcher; queue-scoped roles with the scale rule; the confidential-client flow) and S-ACT-1 (a harmless probe for every Move class). Two platform capabilities the chain leans on are documented and are not the open question: Container Apps scale rules accept a managed identity for Azure Queue Storage (Learn, *Scaling in Azure Container Apps*, Authentication; the `identity` property of a job scale rule is in the 2025-01-01 REST reference), and Azure role assignments can be scoped to a single queue, with ABAC for Azure Queues generally available (Learn, *Authorize access to queues using Azure role assignment conditions*). What stays *to confirm* in the first build sprint is the **combination**: a queue-triggered **job** scaling on a queue-scoped role with the least data-plane right that the scaler needs.
**Round 1** (A-05, A-06, A-07, B-01, B-06, B-08, B-13): **reversed** from "the broker grants and dispatches" — the reviewer's disagreement was accepted.
**Round 2** (B-01, A-01, A-02, A-03, B-06, D-16, E-06; ADR-07 Disagree from B): **reversed again** — the signer no longer starts the runner; the runner is event-driven; the Policy permit and the person's token are verified by the broker and the runner themselves; the "none able to complete a change alone" claim is replaced by the residual above; signers hold custom `acia-signer` roles, not *Key Vault Crypto User*, which also holds `keys/update/action`.
**Third pass** (T-A, TA-01 to TA-17 and TA-25 to TA-28; **Disagree**, high confidence): the chain was still trusting acknowledgements and shared containers. Accepted, with two adjustments of the proposed fixes: the positive decision is bound **into the nonce** instead of a new page-signing key (TA-01); integrity of control items and markers is **item-level signatures** ("only narrowing is unsigned") instead of one container per writer (TA-03, TA-04). Also: two proofs checked by the broker and the runner (TA-02); the broker's residual and the pipeline's are stated, the production pipeline holds no start right and a deployment lock keeps an honest deployment off a live lease (TA-05, TA-06); two-part revocation probe (TA-07); the approval's block is read (TA-08); write-ahead intent and outcome entries (TA-09); credential checks against the credential's own record (TA-10); one residual in every document (TA-11); fail-closed step-up with Conditional Access and defined freshness, no `prompt=login` fallback (TA-12, TA-13); standing read by the broker, none by the runner (TA-14); Verifier reads predictions and the Auditor re-measures (TA-15); private endpoints only in `snet-pe` and VNet ingress for cross-environment callees, both checked on Learn (TA-16, TA-17); queue-processing semantics (TA-18); permit id allocated before the hash (TA-25); the federated credential sits on the app registration (TA-26); pipeline names (TA-27); the kill-switch bound is the 60-second cache (TA-28). T-A would change its mind when forged and duplicated proofs, decline tokens, forged markers, kills at four points, relays and cards all fail closed in a scratch estate; until then the status stays Proposed.
**Closure check** (T-A, second turn at `87925dd`; **Disagree**, high confidence): 8 findings still open and 13 new defects, four Critical. Each platform claim was verified on Learn first (a queue's consumer can put to it, because `messages/write` is a Put Message action; the dequeue count rises on every dequeue; the nonce is chosen by the app; Cosmos DB separates `create` from `replace`). Accepted: the sender sets are stated exactly and a queue is a doorbell (TA-N2); the approve sign-in starts only from a click on Approve (TA-N1); the deployment lock is a two-sided protocol with a fresh re-read, a heartbeat and a job timeout below the lock's time-to-live (TA-N3); the ledger position is a separate marker and an approval record is never amended (TA-N4, TA-08); a create conflict fails closed and is never retried under another id (TA-N5); the sweeper writes nothing and the Verifier records what it removed (TA-N6); the Auditor corroborates savings through its own path (TA-N7); the spine's poison-queue rights are stated (TA-N8); waits are counted by us and the broker's redelivery reconciles (TA-N9, TA-18); registration and arbitration are the facade's (TA-N10, TA-N11, TA-22); freshness is anchored on the ledger's append time (TA-13); the runner re-checks both `oid`s and the broker's hats (TA-02); a fresh read before every step makes the kill-switch claim true (TA-N13); a standing watcher turns a removed approver into a deny entry (TA-14); every Move class needs a harmless probe (TA-07). Adjusted, not adopted as proposed: no receipt-and-hold (the component that serves the page would send it); no signing key for the sweeper (observation instead); no writer-per-container (fail closed instead); no cross-container transaction for the lock (a two-sided protocol instead). The record is [the disposition](../reviews/design-v1-third-pass/disposition.md#6-the-closure-check-at-87925dd-and-the-closure-round).
**Re-check and confirmation** (T-A, third and fourth turns; **Disagree**, the last two defects being in the observer): the lock and the kill switches still rested on a measured replica lag that Session consistency does not bound (TA-M1, Critical), so the operational account's default is now Strong, with Bounded Staleness or a per-read strategy as alternatives and the dependent classes disabled if none holds (C23); the wait counter is signed by the broker, so a counter injected by another sender changes nothing (C25); the Verifier's scheduled observer records what the sweeper removed from a stateless 24-hour window, with a gap alert for a late delivery and a timeout below its schedule so that runs never overlap (C24, C28); and a per-run token that only the wrapper holds, with one late-result rule, closes a same-identity forgery between two runs of one role (C26, C27). T-A would change its mind when the lossless observation and the consistency spike have run; until then the status stays Proposed.

### ADR-08 Approvals are authenticated actions bound to one target
**Status** Proposed (redesigned in Round 2; the third pass and its closure check found further defects and they are fixed in the text, but the sign-in claims and the confidential-client flow are unproven until S-ID-3 and S-ACT-1 have run) · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** published Foundry agents can carry authenticated card actions with target binding.
**Decision.** An approval is a **person's authenticated action** bound to one target **and to the decision**. The default is an Entra-authenticated **web approval page** (public-ingress exception X5) whose sign-in starts only after a click on **Approve** (a decline starts none) and whose OpenID Connect `nonce` is `SHA-256(target hash ‖ "approve" ‖ challenge)`, the challenge being a random value from the Brief: the ID token is stored in the record, so the broker and the runner recompute the nonce and verify the token themselves, and a sign-in made for any other decision never verifies as an approval. A **Teams Adaptive Card** `Action.Execute` with single sign-on approves in place only for the lowest reversible class (the bot channel asserts the user, and there is no end-to-end token bound to the hash); on every other class it opens the page and approves nothing itself. The record binds a target hash that includes the live state and the policy permit, expires, is single-use and is **signed** by the Approval service; a purchase, an irreversible Move that needs two approvers and a credential grant carry **two person proofs**, which the broker and the runner check themselves (two different people, two different hats read live, neither the requester). Step-up is **Conditional Access on the approval app with a multifactor authentication strength** (Entra P1); `auth_time` and `amr` are documented optional claims, requested in the app registration and verified where they arrive, with `auth_time` not earlier than the approval request's creation and `iat` within five minutes of the ledger's append time for the approval's entry, a moment stamped by the Audit Writer and not attested by the Approval service (`exp` is no freshness bound: an ID token lives about an hour); if the claims cannot be obtained the web page is **not used** for irreversible Moves, purchases or credential grants (`prompt=login` with `iat` proves no multifactor and is not a fallback). Approver standing comes only from human-administered Entra groups or RBAC roles on the target, read live by the broker at grant. The page is the person's display, so a compromised page could show one transaction and submit another, or start the approve sign-in without a click (the nonce is chosen by the app, so it proves a sign-in for that decision, not a click): it shows the target's short code, the Brief in the second channel shows the same code for the person to compare before the click, and D13 cross-checks every approval against Entra sign-in logs. Voice, avatar, text and e-mail replies never approve ([07 §4](07-security-architecture.md#4-approvals-that-cannot-be-forged-or-reused)).
**Because.** An approval authorises the only irreversible thing the system does; a model's reading of a chat is not an authorisation, and a signature by the service that relays the click proves nothing about the click.
**Strongest alternative.** Teams' Approvals app (Power Automate) — authenticated and audited, but no target-hash binding or deterministic verification and an added platform; or agent on-behalf-of flows, which Bot-integrated agents cannot use today.
**Consequences.** We run a small bot (public ingress exception X1) in .NET for typing and shared contracts with the Approval service (the Python Agents SDK also registers `Action.Execute` handlers, so the language is a choice, not a constraint); the web page is a second public ingress (X5) and, for most classes, the only approval path.
**Round 1** (B-06, B-07, B-15, E-02): approvals are signed; standing comes from human-administered groups; the web page is declared; the Python claim was wrong and is removed.
**Round 2** (B-01, A-03, B-08, E-01): the person's token is verified by the broker and the runner; Teams approves only the lowest reversible class; `auth_time` and `amr` are **documented** optional claims (B-08's proposal to drop `auth_time` was not adopted).
**Third pass** (T-A, TA-01, TA-02, TA-12, TA-13, TA-14, TA-26; **Disagree**, high confidence): a token proved that a person signed in, not that the person clicked *approve*, so the decision is now bound into the nonce; two-approver proofs are verified by the broker and the runner, not only by the Approval service; the fallback that forced a fresh sign-in and trusted `iat` is deleted (it proves no multifactor); freshness is defined; standing is read by the broker alone; and the federated credential sits on the page's app registration, trusting its managed identity (Learn), with the confidential-client flow *to confirm*. Status stays Proposed.
**Closure check** (T-A; **Disagree**, high confidence): the sequence started the approve sign-in before any click, so a decline could leave a relabellable token (TA-N1, TA-01). The sign-in now starts only from a click on Approve, a decline yields no approve-nonce token, and the residual is said plainly: the nonce proves a sign-in for that decision, not a click, and a compromised page can still induce one. Freshness is anchored on the ledger's append time and not on the Approval service's own word (TA-13), and the runner re-checks both `oid`s from the tokens (TA-02). A receipt-and-hold step was considered and refused, because the component that serves the page would send the receipt. Status stays Proposed.
**Re-check and confirmation** (T-A; **Agree with changes**): the click-bound sign-in is specified, the trusted-display residual is accepted as stated, and S-ID-3 must prove the flow, the claims and the confidential-client assertion. Status stays Proposed.

### ADR-12 The operational store is Cosmos DB serverless
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the vector-index test on serverless fails, or relational integrity becomes decisive.
**Decision.** One Cosmos DB for NoSQL serverless account of our own holds estate knowledge, cases, the Agenda, the Ledger and budget reservations ([06 §7](06-data-and-knowledge.md#7-the-physical-stores)); the spine's orchestration state lives in the Durable Functions task hub ([ADR-33](#adr-33-the-spine-is-durable-functions-on-flex-consumption-calling-leaves-through-the-hub)).
**Because.** No minimum throughput, JSON documents, change feed, and Foundry's own standard setup accepts serverless, so nothing in the design needs a provisioned floor.
**Strongest alternative.** **Azure SQL serverless** — a free offer exists, ledger tables give tamper evidence, and joins are natural; it pauses to storage-only cost but resumes on login with latency. It is the named fallback. PostgreSQL has graph and vector in one engine but is always on.
**Consequences.** Single region (no multi-region serverless); restore relies on an hourly export ([06 §7.6](06-data-and-knowledge.md#76-recovery)). The store has **sixteen containers**, including `leases` (one active lease per execution identity), `controls` (read by every chain service with a cache of at most 60 seconds, a stale cache failing closed; the runner reads it fresh before every step and the broker at grant) and `runs` (the run registry, whose only writer is the case-record facade, so that the evidence in `cases` stays create-only by RBAC).
**Round 2** (A-07): the `leases` container and the chain's read of `controls` were missing.
**Closure check** (T-A, N13 and C21): the 60-second cache bound no longer applies to a Move already running, and the registry that needs a conditional replace moved out of the evidence graph into its own container, which costs nothing on serverless.
**Re-check** (T-A, TA-M1, Critical; C23): the lock and the kill switches rest on one client seeing another client's recent write, and Session consistency gives that only "within a single client session" (a client that never wrote a partition reads it "as reads with Eventual Consistency"), so a measured lag was no guarantee. The account's default consistency is now **Strong**, which "offers a linearizability guarantee" and is available on a single-region account; every reader outside the control plane relaxes to Session per request, and control-plane reads cost half the read throughput, which is negligible here. That Strong works on a serverless account is *to confirm* first in S-ID-3; the fallback is Bounded Staleness (single-region minimum 5 seconds) with a 60-second wait, and if neither is available the classes that need the lock or the kill-switch claim stay disabled.

### ADR-19 Nine layers against injection with a deterministic gate
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** easy · **Revisit when** the Agent Governance Toolkit reaches general availability, or FIDES stabilises.
**Decision.** The nine layers of [07 §5](07-security-architecture.md#5-defence-against-prompt-injection-in-nine-layers), of which layers 0, 1, 6 and 8 are deterministic. Layer 1 is a **source sanitiser** shared by every tool service that returns free text (delimiter escaping, control-character stripping, length caps, an *untrusted* label, chunking at 10,000 characters, the size of the hub's prompt window); the hub's built-in `llm-content-safety` screens MCP and A2A *responses* in windows of **1,000 characters by default** (configurable, with an optional overlap) and prompts in windows of 10,000, with a sliding window for streams, while custom policies must not touch the response body; once untrusted text enters a session, privileged tools are blocked or need a human, and **that taint is enforced in the tool service and at the hub, not in the agent's own process**. The gate before a tool call is the Agent Governance Toolkit **if** it proves out, otherwise an in-house check with the same contract.
**Because.** Probabilistic screening reduces but never eliminates injection; the architecture must not depend on it.
**Strongest alternative.** Relying on Foundry guardrails and Defender alone — several are preview, agent guardrails *override* the model's, document attacks are off by default, and agent-level Defender protection moved behind a licence.
**Consequences.** More code of our own in the gate and the sanitiser; the open-source toolkit has breaking changes between majors. Foundry guardrails moderate only the **first 1,000 characters** of a text input (Learn, service limits), so they are never the control for long untrusted text; the sanitiser's chunking and the hub's screening are. An L5 test asserts that the invocation-protocol moderation setting exists on the deployed path and plants an injection beyond character 1,000 and beyond 10,000.
**Round 1** (B-05, D-03, E-04): the sanitiser covers every free-text route, not only documents; the streaming caveat was wrong for the built-in policy; spike S-GW-4.
**Round 2** (B-04, E-12, E-16): the response window is 1,000 characters, not 10,000; taint is enforced where an injected agent cannot switch it off; per-tool authorisation is *Evaluated* until S-GW-3, with one server per trust class as the generally available fallback.

### ADR-23 Bicep with Verified Modules, Deployment Stacks and GitHub Actions
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** a resource we need has no Bicep type.
**Decision.** Bicep with Azure Verified Modules, subscription-scope Deployment Stacks with deny settings, GitHub Actions with workload identity federation for *privileged* workflows only, self-hosted runners as Container Apps event jobs for private targets; `azd` is the developer's inner loop ([11 §2–3](11-delivery-and-operations.md#2-infrastructure-as-code)). **Agent identities may open pull requests only to data paths** (`atlas/**`, `moves/**` YAML) through a GitHub App installation, whose private key lives in the secrets vault; a GitHub App's permissions are set **per repository, not per path**, so the path limit is the pull-request service's and a required path-guard check's, with a push ruleset that restricts file paths as the candidate third layer (*to confirm* in S-DEL-2); a pull request that touches `moves/**` needs **two approvers who are not the author before it merges**, and a Move's schema has **no executable field** (parameters from enumerations only); workflows triggered by such pull requests run on GitHub-hosted runners with no secrets and no federation, and the `pr-spoke` workflow runs **after the merge**, inside `deploy`, never on the pull request; privileged workflows run from the base branch after a human approves; a required check counts **two distinct approvers who are not the author**. **Because.** Microsoft-first, no state file, native drift protection through stacks.
**Strongest alternative.** Terraform with AzAPI (covers new resource types sooner; the Citadel accelerator has a Terraform twin with no releases) or Azure DevOps (the only pipeline the accelerator ships).
**Consequences.** Stack limits (five excluded principals, resource-group deletion bypass, and moving a managed resource out of a stack removes its protection — detected and covered by a planted-drift test); no module for sandboxes or agent versions, and the Azure Verified Modules index lists Bot Service and the Durable Task Scheduler as *Proposed* (no published module), so they are plain Bicep behind a thin local module. A GitHub environment needs only one of several reviewers, hence the required check; private-repository entitlement is spike S-DEL-2, and with a single operator promotion is limited to shadow and reversible Moves.
**Round 1** (B-04, A-13, E-11).
**Round 2** (B-05, C-09, E-09): `moves/**` needs two approvers before merge and has no executable field; `pr-spoke` runs after the merge; deployment follows the phase profile; the AVM status is stated.

### ADR-24 Keyless by default, platform-managed keys
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** a compliance driver names customer-managed keys.
**Decision.** Managed identities and workload identity federation everywhere; key authentication denied by Policy; platform-managed encryption keys; Key Vault only where a secret cannot be avoided; no Managed HSM ([07 §6](07-security-architecture.md#6-secrets-keys-and-data-protection)). The signing keys of the authority chain and the ledger are the exception that proves the rule: **a vault for each signing party**, a custom **`acia-signer`** role on its own vault (`keys/read` and `keys/sign/action` only — the built-in *Key Vault Crypto User* also holds `keys/update/action` and `keys/backup/action`, so a signer could re-enable a key that a kill switch disabled), and a key-scoped **`acia-key-reader`** role for the verifiers; rotation is one scheduled workflow.
**Because.** Customer-managed keys for Foundry need bring-your-own stores each keyed, hosted-agent `$HOME` cannot be encrypted that way, and Managed HSM costs about €2,000 a month.
**Strongest alternative.** Customer-managed keys everywhere: stronger control posture, a large fixed cost and operational burden. A second alternative for the signing keys, one vault with key-scoped roles, was **not adopted** in Round 2: it saves about €6 a vault and shares one control plane and one diagnostic stream across parties that must not trust each other.
**Consequences.** Adding keys later means re-creating some resources; how the producers' public keys reach each verifier and how they are pinned is spike S-ID-3.
**Round 2** (E-06, A-10, B-01): the custom roles; the vault-per-party design kept on purpose.

### ADR-26 The spine's reservation is the budget; the hub's limits are a second layer
**Status** Accepted with changes (redefined in Round 2; the third pass agreed with changes and they are applied; what the reservation leaves open is measured by S-COST-1) · **Reversibility** easy · **Revisit when** a platform feature enforces a hard spend limit.
**Decision.** The **spine's atomic worst-case euro reservation** before each leaf call is the budget that holds. It covers **every metered part of the call**: tokens at the dearest alias and the dearest *service tier* the contract allows (the hub refuses a caller's own tier, so priority processing is not reachable), the leaf's **own session compute** (vCPU and memory for the role's idle timeout), the Content Safety records the call may add, and the metered tools. It is reserved against the case's and the group's budget; the tokens are reconciled from the hub's usage record and the other parts from the daily cost export, and an actual **above** the reservation is debited to the head, which may go negative (the next reservation against it is refused and D4 raises). The reservation id `<case id>:<step>:<epoch>:<attempt>` makes an activity retry idempotent and a restore conservative (a new epoch double-reserves, never reserves nothing). `max_tokens` is set deliberately and cost per case is a release gate. The **hub's token limits and quotas** per contract and environment are a **second, coarser layer**, **computed from the cost model** (a quota, a starting tokens-per-minute limit, a daily quota where one is declared and an in-flight overrun bound for every contract in every environment where it has planned work, [12](12-cost-and-finops.md)), not typed; Azure budgets alert only.
**Because.** Budgets evaluate daily on old data and stop nothing; quota is pooled per subscription and is not a cost tool; and **tokens are not euros** (cached input, tool calls, sandbox hours, a different model behind an alias and a priority service tier price differently), so a token limit cannot be the budget.
**Strongest alternative.** Budget-triggered automation that disables access: a useful backstop, too slow to be the cap. The Round-1 text made the hub the cap; Round 2's cost lens rejected that, because the printed euro "caps" were blended-price forecasts and a contract that lists Sol could spend Sol's output price on every quota token.
**Consequences.** The hub's `estimate-prompt-tokens` must be on; the hub is the single point whose availability matters. `llm-token-limit` can overshoot when requests run concurrently, so the residual overrun is bounded by a contract's concurrency times its largest call (printed in 12 §2) and is stated, not hidden. The generated table separates the *forecast-equivalent* euros from the *worst case* at the dearest alias; neither is a cap. What stays open until S-COST-1 has run: whether the hub's usage record keeps the reservation id, and how far reserved exceeds billed over the first hundred cases.
**Round 1** (A-08, C-04).
**Round 2** (C-01, C-02, A-06; ADR-26 Disagree from C): the reservation is primary; quotas, limits and bounds are generated for all twelve contracts and checked against 04 §4.3.
**Third pass** (T-B, TB-01 to TB-05; Agree with changes): the reservation now covers hosted-agent compute, screening and tools, not only tokens; an overrun is debited; the id carries the epoch; the hub refuses any caller-supplied service tier (Learn: a request-level `service_tier: priority` is billed at the priority rate on a Global Standard deployment; EU Data Zone Standard does not support priority processing); Pilot has no TEST account, so its exams and gates are quota'd in DEV, and `--check` now fails on a quota for an environment a scenario never creates.

Its fixed cost is larger than the whole compute line only at Pilot scale (€283 against about €170); at Team and Enterprise scale compute is larger, and **renting diagnostics would beat the always-on meter if it saved more than about four crew-hours a month** at €75 an hour, before the active-flow charges that the trial must measure.

### ADR-30 Recovery is redeploy from code with no warm second compute estate
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the operator judges a regional outage intolerable.
**Decision.** RPO ≤ 1 hour for approvals, audit and lessons; RTO one working day; **no warm second compute estate**: the compute is redeployed from code, but the **data is stored twice** — the hourly recovery lake and the daily digests live in a second EU region, and the ledger's account is geo-zone-redundant ([11 §8](11-delivery-and-operations.md#8-resilience-and-recovery)). In-flight cases are rebuilt from the case store (spend and deadlines are persisted there), not from the task hub.
**Because.** The platform has no automatic cross-region failover; a second *compute* estate doubles the always-on floor, while second-region *storage* costs a few euros a month and its copy traffic is priced ([12](12-cost-and-finops.md)).
**Strongest alternative.** A warm second region: faster recovery, roughly doubled fixed cost and a second one-way door.
**Consequences.** Quota and subnet in a second region may be requested and kept dormant, and **the one-working-day RTO assumes capacity is available there**, which a cold drill must show. Restore preserves what must not return: snapshots and tombstones so erased data is not resurrected, revocations kept, approvals older than the restore point invalidated, and a second-region lake that we read from during a regional outage; geo-zone redundancy alone is not a path we can read unless the account is RA-GZRS or fails over ([06 §7.6](06-data-and-knowledge.md#76-recovery)).
**Round 1** (A-12).
**Round 2** (C-11, A-05, C-08, E-10): the ADR says what is absent (a warm compute estate), not "no second region"; budgets and deadlines survive a new task hub; the copy process is priced and tested.

### ADR-32 The audit ledger is hash-chained append blobs in immutable storage
**Status** Accepted with changes · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** immutable append semantics fail the spike S-DATA-2.
**Decision.** Entries are chained append blobs in a container with a locked time-based retention policy; a single **Audit Writer** sequences the entries, and each approval and execution entry is signed by the service that produced it; **the broker, the runner and the Verifier read their own appended block back and match its hash before they proceed**, because the writer's acknowledgement alone proves nothing; blobs roll over before the 50,000-block limit; `allowProtectedAppendWrites` is set *before* the policy is locked (it cannot be changed afterwards); a signed daily digest is anchored in a second account; a nightly job that is **a separate identity and code path from the Audit Writer** verifies the chain ([06 §7.3](06-data-and-knowledge.md#73-the-audit-ledger)). Entries carry **no name, e-mail, UPN, `oid` or Teams id**; the schema rejects them.
**Because.** Tamper-evidence at near-zero cost, in the tenant, with no standing compute.
**Strongest alternative.** Azure Confidential Ledger (about €103 a month for the smallest standard instance; about €289 for a three-node managed CCF): stronger attestation, a standing cost. It is an optional module. SQL ledger tables are a second alternative.
**Consequences.** Not queryable (an index lives in the operational store); erasure works by deleting the pseudonym mapping. No change without a ledger entry: if the Audit Writer is down, no approval is dispatched and no grant is made (signed human approvals are kept and dispatched once their entry commits), while a **rollback to the pre-state named in the signed record is exempt** and runs from a signed outbox, with the gap flagged (D19) and reconciled. The ledger's account is geo-zone-redundant; its second-region copy is the hourly recovery lake.
**Round 1** (B-09).
**Round 2** (B-06, B-03, A-10, E-10): read-back by the producers; an independent nightly verifier; no personal identifiers in an entry; the rollback exemption; the GZRS wording.

### ADR-33 The spine is Durable Functions on Flex Consumption, calling leaves through the hub
**Status** Proposed (leaf-call rule redesigned in Round 2; the third pass and its closure check agreed with changes and they are applied, but the rule stays unproven until spikes S-HOST-1 and S-ORCH-1 have run) · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** spike S-ORCH-1 fails, or the Durable Extension for the Agent Framework is generally available *and* beats Durable Functions in that spike.
**Decision.** The spine is **Durable Functions** (C#, isolated worker) on the **Flex Consumption** plan with the Azure Storage provider; the Durable Task Scheduler (its Consumption SKU is generally available since April 2026, though Learn's billing page still says preview) is a drop-in upgrade if the provider proves too slow. Orchestrators are deterministic and call no model: activities start leaf runs *through the hub* and return a run id; orchestrators wait for external events (from the Approval service and the chain through the `spine-events` queue, from the Brief service and the case-record facade through `spine-signals`) racing a deadline timer. **A leaf's completion is its terminal `run-result` record**, written through the case-record facade and bound to the caller's verified identity, the case, the step, the invocation and the epoch; the leaf's **worker** (not its HTTP handler, which returns at once) makes that call in its `finally`, and the facade then sends one `run-completed` event; the orchestrator re-reads the record and **ignores an event with no record behind it**, discards an event of another epoch, and leaves the arbitration with the deadline timer to the **case-record facade**: the spine registers a run and closes it at its deadline through the facade (the app role `Run.Manage`, held by the spine alone), and the facade moves the run's registry item in `runs` by conditional replace (`registered`, `claimed`, `started`, then `completed`, `timed-out` or `abandoned`), so that the first write wins, a late result is stored as `late` and never reopens an attempt, and a claim that never starts is released. The item carries the outcome summary and the facade creates the `run-result` record from it, idempotently; any call that finds a `completed` item with no record creates it, so a crash between the two writes strands nothing. A repeated call finds the run-registry item and returns the earlier record instead of starting a second run. Competing hypotheses fan out and in; instance ids `<workflow>:<case id>` (a case id of at most 48 characters, the composed id within Learn's 100-character limit and its forbidden characters, `:<epoch>` appended on a restore) are idempotency keys; the spine reserves each call's worst-case cost atomically before it starts ([ADR-26](#adr-26-the-spines-reservation-is-the-budget-the-hubs-limits-are-a-second-layer)), and persists per-case spend and timer deadlines in the case store so that a restore can rebuild open cases under a new instance id. A hosted leaf's **idle timeout is at least its role's p99 active time**, because the platform deprovisions compute when no request arrives within the timeout; spike S-HOST-1 decides the fallbacks (hold the request open with response buffering off, or the Responses protocol's background mode). The Agent Framework runs only *inside* leaves ([05 §4](05-agents-and-orchestration.md#4-how-the-spine-runs)).
**Because.** It is generally available, it scales to zero (nothing runs while a person decides), it runs under the spine's own identity outside any agent boundary, and it supplies the checkpointing, timers, external events, fan-out and versioning that our own design would have to build, test and secure. Learn lists Flex Consumption as fully supported for both providers.
**Strongest alternative.** **Our own checkpoint-and-jobs design** — Agent Framework graph workflows as short-lived Container Apps jobs with a custom checkpoint store in Cosmos DB — was the draft's choice. It is now the **named fallback** and would need JSON-only state, an inbox and outbox, lease fencing and idempotent effects. A workflow inside a hosted agent would run the spine inside a leaf's identity; the Durable Extension for the Agent Framework is pre-release and is not used.
**Consequences.** C# for the spine ([ADR-36](#adr-36-c-for-the-spine-the-execution-plane-and-the-teams-adapter-python-for-agents)); one always-ready instance for the `durable` group (about €21 a month; always-ready billing has no free grants, and Learn requires at least two instances per group if zone redundancy is enabled, which the design does not enable at Pilot and Team scale) and queue polling at 10 seconds or less, both from Learn's Flex guidance; orchestrator code must stay deterministic; **orchestration versioning for in-flight instances is a preview** (release record 498144), so the fallback is a new orchestrator function name per breaking change with the old one drained; long waits are loops of timers. Lock-in to Durable Functions semantics; the storage provider is swappable.
**Round 1** (A-10, D-04): **reversed**. The draft weighed its own design against the wrong alternative — the pre-release extension for the Agent Framework — instead of the generally available Durable Functions runtime beneath it.
**Round 2** (A-04, E-05, A-05, E-04, E-08): the leaf-call rule, the terminal event, a restore that rebuilds cases from the case store, versioning on the preview register, and the DTS SKU status.
**Third pass** (T-A, TA-18 to TA-24; Agree with changes, high confidence): completion is a persisted terminal record, not an event (every role writes a `run-result`, including the Conductor, the Examiner and the Forger); the event is emitted by the worker, not the handler that returns at once; the facade checks the caller's verified identity against the run the spine registered; the wrapper's idempotency is ours (the Invocations adapter only echoes the id); records and events carry the workflow and the epoch; the instance-id budget respects Learn's limits; the hold-open fallback turns response buffering off.
**Closure check** (T-A, TA-20 to TA-22, TA-N10 and TA-N11; Agree with changes): the spine's authority to register a run was not in the role matrix, and "the first of a result and the deadline wins" was not atomic. Both are now the facade's: one app role for the spine, and a registry item whose every transition is a conditional replace; a claim that never starts is released by the spine's reconcile. The registry sits in its own container so that the evidence graph stays create-only by RBAC, and the event is sent by the facade on the worker's call, since only the facade holds the send right. The status stays Proposed until S-HOST-1 and S-ORCH-1 have run, as before.
**Re-check and confirmation** (T-A; Disagree at the re-check, then **Agree with changes**): per-run authorisation and a single late-result rule were missing. The facade now requires a per-run token that only the leaf's wrapper holds (it stores only the token's SHA-256, and two runs of one role share an identity but not a token); one late-result rule is worded the same in 03, 04 and 05; and the registry item carries the outcome, so that a crash between the facade's two writes is repaired by the next call. S-ORCH-1 proves the token's isolation and the late-result recovery.

### ADR-36 C# for the spine, the execution plane and the Teams adapter; Python for agents
**Status** Accepted · **Reversibility** moderate · **Revisit when** the Foundry .NET SDK and hosting reach general availability with parity, or the Z3 dependency surface changes.

**Round 1** (E-02, A-10).

### ADR-37 Model-written code runs only in a sandbox with no identity
