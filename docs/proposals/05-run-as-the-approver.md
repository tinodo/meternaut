# Run as the approver — a decision record

**Status: ordered by the owner on 2026-11-05; built, deployed and proved once on the real estate the same evening (the owner's own approval; the Activity Log names him as the caller).** This page records one decision: Meternaut holds no right to change anything in Azure. A change is made by the person who approves it, with that person's own rights, while that person is signed in. It replaces the writer of [the loop](04-the-loop.md) (see "The problem") and removes the write role of that identity from the [access requirements](03-access-and-onboarding.md).

## The problem

Until this decision, a change was made by a writer: the managed identity `id-meternaut-runner`. It held one custom role in the project's own resource group (requirement W1): read and delete a managed disk, read and delete a public IP address, and read management locks. That is five actions in one resource group. It was not Owner and it was not Contributor. After a person approved a plan, it waited 120 seconds, in which the person could cancel, and then it deleted the resource. Two real approvals went through it on 2026-11-05.

The owner rejected this design on the same day. His objection: if Meternaut's own identity carries out approved changes, that identity needs Owner-like rights on whatever it may change, and no customer will grant that. The change has to be made on behalf of the person who approves it.

The objection still applies to a role that narrow, for three reasons:

1. **It is a standing right.** The identity holds the role at every moment, not only while a person approves. Any code that runs as the app can use it.
2. **It grows with every kind of change.** A resize or a stop needs write actions on virtual machines, and a purchase needs a purchase right. Each is one more role at each scope that may be changed, which the owner of that scope has to grant and review. W1 was one role on a group that Meternaut owns itself; the same role on a customer's subscription is a write right for a tool.
3. **It conflicts with two Microsoft references.** The Well-Architected Framework asks to limit access to what is necessary and to give it just in time and just enough (security recommendation SE:05, section "Make conditional access choices", [Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/well-architected/security/identity-access)). The Microsoft cloud security benchmark asks to avoid standing access and to give temporary, time-bound permissions instead of persistent ones (control PA-2, [Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/security/benchmark/azure/mcsb-v2-privileged-access)).

The earlier proposals presented the writer as the design. It was a pilot shortcut. It worked because the pilot's resource group belongs to Meternaut and to nobody else, so nobody had to grant anything. It does not carry to an estate that belongs to somebody else.

## The options

Five ways to carry out an approved change were compared. The question for each is which identity holds the right to change Azure, and when.

| Option | How it works | What Meternaut holds | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A standing writer with a role limited to a few actions** | A managed identity of Meternaut holds a custom role at the scope of the change. After an approval it makes the change on its own, optionally after a delay | A write role at every scope that may be changed, at all times | **Rejected.** It is a standing write right of a tool identity. Each new kind of change adds rights at the customer's scopes, and the owner of each scope has to grant and review them. Its one advantage is that it can run without a person present: a delay with a cancel button, or a change at a set time |
| **Run as the approver, with a delegated token** | The approver is sent to Microsoft Entra to sign in for Azure when approving. The console redeems the code and makes the change with that person's access token, in the same request, and keeps nothing | No write right. One delegated permission of the app registration, which works only for a signed-in person | **Chosen.** Azure checks the person's own rights, so Meternaut can do no more than that person can. The Activity Log names the person. The tenant's own Conditional Access applies to the approval. A new kind of change adds no right |
| **On-behalf-of from the platform's token store** | App Service authentication keeps the signed-in person's tokens in its token store. The console exchanges one of them for an Azure token with the on-behalf-of flow | No write right | **Not chosen.** The effect is the same, and it needs more. The on-behalf-of flow takes a token whose audience is the console itself, so the console has to expose an API of its own ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-on-behalf-of-flow)). The token store holds an access token only for a provider that is configured with a client secret ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/app-service/configure-authentication-oauth-tokens)), and this sign-in has none ([rule 23](../../AGENTS.md)). The token store also keeps the person's tokens beyond one request |
| **Just-in-time elevation of Meternaut's identity with Privileged Identity Management (PIM)** | The identity holds the write role only for a period that starts when a person approves | The write role, during that period | **Not chosen.** The tool identity still holds the write right during the period, and code that runs as the app can use it then. PIM cannot make an application, a service principal or a managed identity eligible, because it cannot perform the activation steps ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/role-based-access-control/pim-integration)). What remains is a role assignment that a person, or a second identity, creates for each change. That needs a right to assign roles, which is a larger right than the one it replaces ([access and onboarding](03-access-and-onboarding.md)). PIM also needs a Microsoft Entra ID P2 or Microsoft Entra ID Governance licence |
| **A pipeline of the customer that carries out a proposed change with the customer's own identity** | Meternaut writes the change as a proposal in the customer's own repository or pipeline. The customer's pipeline carries it out with a workload identity that the customer controls | No right in Azure. A place to put the proposal | **A later family.** It fits an estate that is managed as code: the change goes through the review and the pipeline that the customer already has. It does not fit a resource that code does not describe. Not built |

## The decision

Run as the approver. Meternaut holds no right to change anything in Azure. It makes a change only as a signed-in person, with that person's rights, while that person is present. The decision adds no Azure resource, so it adds no cost.

- **What Meternaut holds.** Reads of the estate (the identity of the Watch: Reader, Cost Management Reader and the other requirements of [access and onboarding](03-access-and-onboarding.md)) and writes to its own storage. Nothing else in Azure.
- **What is retired.** The identity `id-meternaut-runner`, its custom role (W1) and its one role assignment. Removing what exists in Azure is a change like any other: it goes through a workflow or the owner's written word ([rule 18](../../AGENTS.md)), and the changelog records it.
- **What stays.** A plan is made only for a resource that Meternaut planted itself (it carries a tag), in the project's own resource group. No resource of another project is ever a target. This guard is now a choice and not a need: a person acts with their own rights, so nobody has to grant Meternaut a right before it may touch more. Relaxing the guard is a later decision of the owner.
- **What this does not cover.** The deployment workflow has an identity of its own. It deploys Meternaut's resources in the project's group on a run that the owner approves ([rule 18](../../AGENTS.md)). The running system does not use it.

## The flow

An approval takes two requests of the browser. The first leaves the console for Microsoft Entra, and the second comes back. The protocol is the OAuth 2.0 authorization code flow with PKCE, for a web app with a delegated permission ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-auth-code-flow)).

1. **Approve.** The person presses "Approve and sign in to Azure" on the plan page. The form carries the plan's code and a one-use token. The console checks the person's role, that the person is a named approver of the plan, that the plan is open, the code, the one-use token, the switches and the expiry, and records no decision yet. It makes a one-use `state`, a PKCE verifier and a nonce, and keeps them in the store for 10 minutes, bound to the person, the plan and the resource that was approved. It answers `303` to the Microsoft Entra authorize endpoint of the tenant, for the console's own app registration, with:
   - the scope `openid profile https://management.azure.com/user_impersonation`, and no `offline_access`. `profile` is there on purpose: Microsoft Entra puts the object id (`oid`) in the ID token only with it ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/id-token-claims-reference));
   - the redirect address `https://<console host>/arm/return`;
   - the S256 challenge of the verifier, the `state`, the nonce and a login hint, and no `prompt`.
2. **Sign in to Azure.** Entra signs the person in for Azure management. With a session at Microsoft Entra this is silent: the press of "Approve and sign in to Azure" is the approval. Whether Entra asks again (password, second factor) is decided by the tenant's own Conditional Access and by Azure's own multifactor rule, not by Meternaut. Entra shows a consent prompt once if the tenant needs it, then sends the browser to `GET /arm/return?code=...&state=...`.
3. **Redeem.** The console takes the `state` from the store: once, and only for the person it was made for. It checks the plan again (state, expiry, code and switches). It redeems the code at the token endpoint with the PKCE verifier. As its credential it presents a client assertion: a token of the managed identity `id-meternaut-console-auth` for `api://AzureADTokenExchange`, which the federated credential of the app registration trusts ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/workload-id/workload-identity-federation-config-app-trust-managed-identity)). There is no secret and no certificate ([rule 23](../../AGENTS.md)). It checks the ID token ("The ID token", below). If a check fails, nothing is recorded.
4. **Record and re-check.** The console claims the plan's action once and reads the kill switch again (an approval that is in the middle of its sign-in when changes are paused stops here). Only then does it record the approval. With the person's access token it reads the resource and the management locks again and checks every condition of the plan. It stops at the first one that fails, and nothing changes.
5. **Change.** It reads the kill switch again right before the delete. It deletes the resource once, without a retry, and reads until Azure answers that the resource is gone (up to six reads, five seconds apart). The access token is held in memory for this one request. It is not stored, not logged and not shown on a page. There is no refresh token.
6. **Azure decides.** Azure checks the person's own rights on every call. If the person may not delete the resource, Azure answers 403, nothing changes, the plan closes as failed for every approver and the page says so in plain words. If Azure answers 401 with a claims challenge instead, the person's token lacks a check that a policy asks for, and nothing was deleted: see "When Azure asks for another check", below. The Activity Log shows the person as the caller and the console's app registration as the client application (proved on 2026-11-05).
7. **Tell and check.** The page that the person has come back to says what happened. The timer sends the letter about the change. Later it reads the resource again, and two days later the cost lines, with an identity that only reads.

**When Azure asks for another check.** A person who signed in without the multifactor check that a change needs can open the console, and the first sign-in then ends with a token that Azure Resource Manager refuses for the delete: it answers 401 with a `www-authenticate` header (the scheme Bearer, the error `insufficient_claims` and a `claims` directive that holds a claims request). Microsoft's documentation of the identity platform tells a client to send the person back to Microsoft Entra with that claims request and to try again ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/claims-challenge)), and the console does that, once:

1. Nothing was deleted, so the approval stays recorded, the plan stays approved and its action goes back to the queue, marked as waiting for the check. The page of the plan says so in plain words.
2. The console sends the person to Entra once more, in a state of its own, with a new PKCE verifier and a new nonce, and the claims request in the `claims` parameter of the authorize address (the same check applies to this address as to the first: it must be the Entra sign-in address). The claims request waits in the state of that sign-in, for ten minutes at most, and nowhere else.
3. The return of that sign-in records no second approval. It checks that the person's approval is recorded for this plan and code and that the action waits for the check, that the plan is still approved and that changes are not paused. It redeems the code with the claims request too, checks the ID token as before, and makes the delete once more with every check of the plan made again.
4. A second claims challenge, a cancel, an Entra error, a Conditional Access refusal, an ID token of another account, or a check that is not completed within ten minutes of the recorded approval (the timer closes the waiting action) close the action and the plan as failed, with the reason in plain words. Nothing was changed. Meternaut asks for the check once: a new plan follows when the Watch finds the resource again.

The header comes from outside, so it is read by the runner alone and strictly: it has to be a Bearer challenge of quoted directives, with the error `insufficient_claims` and one `claims` directive of at most 2000 bytes (base64 or base64url) that hold one JSON object of whole numbers, true, false, null, printable ASCII text and at most four levels and twenty keys. Anything else, a repeated name included, is an ordinary refusal. The header is cut at 1500 characters, is not part of any error text and reaches no log, no line of the History, no page and no stored record.

**What the timer does now.** It runs every minute with the reader's identity, and its list of allowed calls holds three reads, so it cannot change Azure. It closes the plans whose time is over, marks stuck actions (one that never started, or that has run for more than 15 minutes), closes an action that waited more than ten minutes for the extra sign-in check, checks afterwards that the resource is gone and, two days later, that its cost lines stopped, sends letters and removes used tokens.

**What is removed, and what is kept.** The 120-second delay and the Cancel button are removed. A delayed run needs a token that is stored until the run starts, and this design stores none. The press of "Approve and sign in to Azure" is the confirmation: the page says in plain words that the change runs right after it and cannot be undone. Decline, ask a question and "not mine" are kept.

**In the code.** The new module `src/case-functions/cases/delegated.py` takes the two steps of an approval: `POST /ok/<plan>` starts it and `GET /arm/return` finishes it. The settings `CHANGE_RESOURCE_GROUP`, `CHANGE_SUBSCRIPTION_ID`, `SIGNIN_TENANT_ID` and `SIGNIN_CLIENT_ID` are new. (On 2026-11-08 the first two became one list, `CHANGE_SCOPES`, and an approval gained more families: [families of change](14-families-of-change.md).) The two sign-in settings are set together with the platform's `OVERRIDE_USE_MI_FIC_ASSERTION_CLIENTID`, which names the identity that presents the assertion, or not at all; without them the loop works and nobody can approve a change. `WRITER_RESOURCE_GROUP`, `WRITER_SUBSCRIPTION_ID`, `WRITER_CLIENT_ID` and `APPROVAL_DELAY_SECONDS` are gone. The Content-Security-Policy `form-action` is now `'self' https://login.microsoftonline.com` (the reason is in [the loop](04-the-loop.md#safety)).

## Security analysis

- **What the token can do.** The access token is for Azure Resource Manager and carries the person's rights. Azure checks the person's role assignments on every call, so the token can do what that person can do and nothing more. The permission `user_impersonation` gives the app no right of its own: a call succeeds only when the app has the scope and the person has the right ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/delegated-access-primer)). Azure does not limit the token to a disk or an IP address. The code does (see "The allow-list").
- **Where the token lives.** In memory, for the one request that makes the change. It is never logged, stored or shown on a page or in a letter. The scope has no `offline_access`, so Entra gives no refresh token ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-auth-code-flow)) and nothing can renew the access token. When the request ends, the app holds nothing that lets it act as that person again. A copy of the token would work at Azure until it expires. The app makes no copy.
- **The allow-list.** The code may use the token for a list of five calls only: read and delete a managed disk, read and delete a public IP address, and read management locks, in the one resource group of the one subscription. A call outside the list is refused before it leaves the app, and the app's HTTP client does not follow redirects, because a redirect would send the person's token to the new address. The list is code; Azure does not enforce it. Code that runs as the app during an approval could use the token for everything that the approver may do at Azure, until the token expires. The writer's right existed at all times and covered five actions in one group; this right exists only during an approval and covers what that person may do. That is the trade that this decision makes.
- **State, PKCE and nonce.** The `state` is random, used once, valid for 10 minutes and bound to the person's object id, the plan and the resource that was approved. A return with an unknown, used or expired `state`, with the `state` of another person, or for a plan that names another resource, records nothing and changes nothing. PKCE ties the code to this console and this request: the verifier stays on the server, and only its S256 challenge reaches the browser. The nonce comes back in the ID token and must match.
- **The redirect address.** One fixed address, built from the console's configured host name and not from the request. That host name is the console's host name on the platform's Application Gateway when the secret `CONSOLE_HOST_NAME` of the environment `hub` is set, and the app's own name when it is not ([phase 3 for the cases app](07-private-by-default.md#publishing-code-to-the-private-apps-phase-3-for-the-cases-app-the-console-behind-the-platforms-application-gateway-built-not-deployed)); the app registration holds the return address of whichever is in use. Entra accepts a redirect address only when it matches one that is registered on the app registration exactly ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-auth-code-flow)). In the other direction, the console redirects only to the Microsoft Entra sign-in address and refuses any other.
- **What a stolen or recorded `state` or code gives.** The code and the `state` are in the URL of `GET /arm/return`, which the platform may record in its request telemetry (not verified). A `state` alone gives nothing: it names a record that holds no token, and it works once, for the person it was made for. A code alone gives nothing: redeeming it needs the PKCE verifier, which stays on the server, and the console's own client assertion, so nobody else can redeem a recorded code. A code lives about a minute ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-auth-code-flow)), and Entra refuses a second redemption (error AADSTS54005, [Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/reference-error-codes)). Somebody who can read the store's tables can read a pending verifier for up to ten minutes, and still lacks the code and the credential. The access token is never logged, stored or shown. The first real run checks what the telemetry holds: a query over `requests` for the `/arm/return` URL.
- **The ID token.** The console checks the issuer (this tenant's v2.0 endpoint), the expiry, the tenant, the audience (this client), the nonce and the object id (`oid`) against the person who is signed in to the platform. The `oid` is there because the scope has `profile`. Another account is refused with a plain message and records nothing. The console does not check the token's signature: the token comes from the token endpoint over TLS (the back channel), in answer to a request that carries the console's own credential, and OpenID Connect allows that ([OpenID Connect Core, 3.1.3.7](https://openid.net/specs/openid-connect-core-1_0.html#IDTokenValidation)).
- **Replay and races.** The first press uses up the form's one-use token, and the `state` works once: a repeated `GET /arm/return` finds no `state` and changes nothing, and a second answer to an approved plan is refused as already answered. A HEAD request to `/arm/return` is refused, so a prefetch cannot use up a sign-in. The action of a plan is claimed once: two approvers who answer at the same moment cannot both record an approval or both delete, and the second sees that the plan is being approved at this moment and records nothing.
- **The tenant's own controls.** The app asks for a token for Azure management, so Conditional Access policies that target that cloud app apply to the approval ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/architecture/secure-resource-management#conditional-access)). Azure also requires multifactor authentication from a user account for a create, update or delete through the control-plane REST API, rolled out to tenants gradually since 1 October 2025 ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity/authentication/concept-mandatory-multifactor-authentication)). The request has no `prompt=login`, which would ask the person again every time ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/v2-oauth2-auth-code-flow)). So Entra finishes the sign-in without a prompt when the person's session already meets the policies; when it does not, Entra asks, and nothing is approved until the person has answered.
- **The record.** The History holds `signin.started`, `signin.stepup` (Azure asked for another check, and the person was sent to Entra once more), `signin.failed` (with a reason, never a token), `approval.recorded`, `action.started` and `action.finished`.
- **The claims challenge.** The `www-authenticate` header of a 401 is outside input. The runner reads it strictly and fails closed (see "When Azure asks for another check"), and a header that passes only lets the console send the person to Entra with a claims request that Azure itself gave. A forged or odd header gives an ordinary refusal and no second sign-in. The second sign-in is bound as the first is (the person's object id, the plan, the code, the resource, a `state` that works once, PKCE, a nonce), the approval is not recorded twice, the check cannot be asked for a second time, and the person's token still reaches the five allowed calls only.
- **Who can produce a token.** The administrator's consent covers the whole tenant. Only people who are assigned to the console's enterprise application can sign in to it, so only they can produce a token for the app.
- **What this does not stop.** An approver who approves the wrong plan: the plan shows the exact change, written by code, and reading it is the approver's part. Code that runs as the app during an approval (see "The allow-list"). A person who holds rights over many resources at Azure and approves a wrong plan.

## What changes for a customer

| What | With the writer | Run as the approver |
|---|---|---|
| A right to change Azure for a tool identity | A custom role for `id-meternaut-runner` (W1), and one more role at each scope for each new kind of change | None. The identity, the role and the assignment are retired |
| Reads for the Watch (R1 to R5) | Granted | The same |
| The app registration of the console | Sign-in only: the permission User.Read, with the administrator's consent | Also one delegated permission, Azure Service Management `user_impersonation`, and a second redirect address, `/arm/return`. The administrator's consent is needed where the tenant does not let people consent for themselves |
| What the tenant's administrator decides | The sign-in, and a write role at every scope that may change | The sign-in, and consent for the one delegated permission. The permission gives Meternaut no standing right: it can act only as a signed-in person, with that person's rights, while that person is present |
| What an approver needs | The role approver in the console's list | The same, and their own right in Azure to make the change. Meternaut cannot give it |
| Conditional Access and multifactor authentication | Not involved: the change came from a tool identity | Applied when Entra signs the approver in for Azure. Entra asks again only when the policies require it |
| The Activity Log shows | `id-meternaut-runner` | The approver, and the console's app registration as the client application (proved on 2026-11-05) |
| Delay and cancel | 120 seconds, with a Cancel button | None. The press of "Approve and sign in to Azure" is the confirmation, and the change cannot be undone |
| Changes without a person present | Possible in principle: a queued action ran by itself | Not possible |

After this decision Meternaut holds these rights: reads of the estate, writes to its own storage, the right to send mail as one shared mailbox (Exchange's role-based access for applications, limited by a management scope), and one delegated permission that works only with a signed-in person. It holds no right to change anything in Azure. The hand steps are in [what a person has to do once](04-the-loop.md#what-a-person-has-to-do-once).

## Known limits

- **The person must hold the right.** This is intended. A person who may not delete the resource gets a 403 from Azure, nothing changes and the page says so.
- **Only changes that fit in a request.** A request has a time limit. It waits for Azure to delete a disk or a public IP address, which takes seconds. A change that takes minutes, such as a resize of a virtual machine, needs a design of its own. It is not built.
- **No scheduled change.** A run needs a person present. A change at a chosen time, a change on a schedule and a change under a standing permission (a rule that lets the system act without a new OK, see [autonomy is earned](../design/03-agents-and-workflows.md#7-autonomy-is-earned)) are not possible with this design. Delegated access is a poor fit for work that must run without a signed-in person ([Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/entra/identity-platform/delegated-access-primer)).
- **Consent.** A tenant that blocks user consent needs one administrator consent for the one delegated permission. Until it is given, the page says so and nothing is approved.
- **Multifactor authentication may be demanded at the approval.** Conditional Access, and Azure's own rule for changes, can ask the person for a second factor. This is intended.
- **When Azure refuses the change.** If Azure answers 403, the plan closes as failed for every approver, and a new plan follows on a later run. A claims challenge that asks for another multifactor sign-in is not a refusal: the person goes through Entra once more and the change is tried once more (see "When Azure asks for another check"). A second challenge closes the plan as failed.
- **A store failure.** If the store fails between the claim on the plan's action and the recorded decision, the console releases the claim on a best-effort basis.
- **Purchases and two approvers.** A purchase needs the right to buy, and Meternaut holds no such right: under this design the person who makes the purchase must hold it. For a change that is larger than a rule says, two people can be required (off by default; built and tested against fakes, not run in Azure): the first confirms the plan's digest with no sign-in to Azure, and a second, different person approves and signs in with their own rights, so the change is made with the second person's rights and Azure checks them. The first person's confirmation changes nothing and carries no token. Several approvals are not carried by one sign-in: each approval keeps its own sign-in, bound to one plan's digest ([the loop](04-the-loop.md#going-through-the-requests-quiet-hours-and-two-people)).
- **Teams.** A Teams card cannot carry the sign-in to Azure. It opens the plan page ([Teams and Outlook](13-teams-and-outlook.md)).
- **The pilot guard is a choice.** The design no longer needs it for security. Relaxing it is a later decision of the owner.
- **Proved once, not in every case.** On 2026-11-05 the owner approved a planted disk: Entra signed him in with multifactor, the console recorded the approval and deleted the disk with his token, and the Activity Log names him as the caller of the delete and the console's app registration as the client application (`appidacr` 2, scope `user_impersonation`). The request telemetry holds no query string for `/arm/return`. The two approvals before that went through the writer. A refusal by Azure (403), a sign-in that needs consent and a Conditional Access refusal have not been seen on the real estate, and the step-up for a claims challenge is built and tested with fakes only (the header, the second sign-in and the retry), not seen at the real Azure or the real Entra.
