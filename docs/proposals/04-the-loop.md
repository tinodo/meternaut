# The loop — a proposal

**Status: approved on 2026-11-05 (the owner: "GO", for the whole solution), built and deployed the same day. The way a change is made was replaced on 2026-11-05 by [run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md), which is built, deployed and proved once on the real estate. A page below is true of the real environment only as far as the changelog says that it was checked there: Watches made plans for planted disks, the approval letters reached the owner, the console answers on an always-on Basic plan, and the owner approved three plans on the page. The first two were deleted by a separate identity, the writer (`id-meternaut-runner`), now retired and removed; the third was deleted as the approver, and the Activity Log names the owner as the caller. The check two days later is next.** This page says how a case that the Watch finds becomes a person's answer and, for the one kind of resource that Meternaut may touch, a checked change to Azure: the console, the approval, the change that the approver makes, the mail, the resources that Meternaut plants itself, and what stays with a person. It follows the [first cases](02-first-cases.md), whose "Not built, and next" this is, and it builds on the [access requirements](03-access-and-onboarding.md). The pages are designed in [the web pages](../experience/06-web-pages.md).

## What it is for

Until now Meternaut found leads, two AI roles judged them and the History recorded the work, and nothing reached a person except by opening a portal workbook. A workbook is a poor first contact: it reads its data from the browser, so a network that resolves the Azure Monitor query addresses to a private IP address (a corporate VPN, for instance) shows "Query failed", and the person has nobody to answer to. The loop closes that gap:

1. a case that is proposed for deletion becomes a plan that **code** writes, with its evidence, what could go wrong and how to undo it;
2. the person who may approve it gets a letter with a link;
3. the person signs in, reads the plan on a page and answers it;
4. right after the person presses "Approve and sign in to Azure", the console makes the change **as that person**, with that person's own rights, after it has read the resource again;
5. later the result is checked against Azure and against the cost lines, and the person is told.

Only a resource that Meternaut planted itself, in its own resource group, can reach step 4. Everything else is still reported and never changed: no other project's resource is a target ([rule 26 of AGENTS.md](../../AGENTS.md)). That is a choice and not a need of the design ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md)), and relaxing it is a later decision of the owner: the pilot keeps the guard so that the first real approvals touch only resources that nobody else owns.

## The path of one case

| Step | What happens | Who or what does it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Find | The Watch (daily, or when the control hub is asked) lists leads, puts the waste that Meternaut planted itself first (it is the one kind that a plan is made for), drops the ones judged in the last seven days so that the slots of a run go to new leads, and takes up to three. Since 2026-11-06 an Investigator agent in Foundry also looks through the estate by itself, with generic read-only tools, and each resource of its verified findings becomes a case ([the Foundry agents](06-foundry-agents.md)) | The Watch, as before |
| 2. Judge | Two AI roles on two different models, then the guards in code (lock, keep tag, too new, managed group). A planted resource is not held back for being new, and the models are told so: they see the typed fact `planted` and no age, because a disk of age zero made the Skeptic ask whether it was only just made (the first real run on a planted disk ended as "needs a person" for that reason, and no plan was made). Every other lead is shown as before, and every other guard still holds. The findings of the Foundry Investigator pass the same guards, a Skeptic agent that tries to break them, and the same decision in code | The Watch |
| 3. Record | Each case is written to the store with its steps, models and facts. A case that is proposed for deletion of an unattached managed disk or an unassigned public IP address, with the planted tag, in the project's own resource group (the setting `CHANGE_RESOURCE_GROUP`), gets a **plan** | The Watch, then the approvals code |
| 4. Tell | A letter to each person who may approve: the first letter to a person says who Meternaut is and what it will and will not do; the approval letter has the link `https://<console>/ok/<plan>` | The mailer, as the shared mailbox |
| 5. Answer | The person signs in with Microsoft Entra and opens the page. It shows the exact change, the evidence level, what could go wrong, how to undo it and the saving. The answers: approve, decline, ask a question, or "not mine". The form carries the plan's 8-character code and a one-use token, so an answer to a plan that has changed, or a form that was used already, is refused. Declining, asking and "not mine" are recorded at once. **Approve and sign in to Azure** records nothing yet: the page says in plain words that Microsoft Entra signs the person in to Azure next, that the change runs right after it and that it cannot be undone | The person |
| 6. Sign in to Azure | Approve sends the person to Microsoft Entra to sign in for Azure management ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md)). With a session at Entra the sign-in is silent; whether Entra asks again is the tenant's choice (Conditional Access, Azure's own multifactor rule), not Meternaut's. Entra asks for consent once where the tenant needs it, and returns the person to the console with a one-use code. The console checks the `state` of the request, redeems the code with the app registration's federated credential, and checks the ID token: issuer, expiry, tenant, this client, the nonce, and that its object id (`oid`) is the one of the person who is signed in to the console | The person, Microsoft Entra and the console |
| 7. Change | Only now the console claims the plan's action (a second approver at the same moment records nothing), reads the switches again and records the approval. With the person's own token it re-reads the resource and the locks, checks every precondition again, reads the switches once more, deletes the resource once and reads until Azure says that it is gone. Azure checks the person's own rights: if the person may not delete the resource, Azure refuses and nothing changes. The token is held in memory for this one request | The console, as the approver |
| 8. Tell again | A letter says what ran and what the outcome was | The mailer |
| 9. Check | Two days later the timer reads the resource again (it must still be gone) and the cost lines of the resource's cost scope, with an identity that only reads; the case becomes `verified`, and a last letter says so | The timer |

The plan's words (title, what changes, what could go wrong, how to undo it, the evidence level) are written by code from the lead's typed facts. A model contributes only the objections that the Skeptic raised, shown in the notebook, and never a sentence of the plan. The plan's code is the first 8 characters of the SHA-256 of the plan id, the family, the resource id and the expiry time: a changed plan has a new code, so an old approval cannot be reused for it.

## What is built

| Part | What it is | Identity and rights |
|---|---|---|
| The store | Ten tables of the usage recording's storage account (runs, cases, plans, decisions, actions, switches, settings, notes, one-use tokens, mail records), written over HTTPS with the Azure Table REST API. The app's list of allowed calls holds the account's table address and nothing else for this part. A one-use token (of a form, or the `state` of an approval's sign-in) is stored as a hash, never as the token. The record of a `state` also holds the PKCE verifier and the nonce of that sign-in for ten minutes at most, and it is deleted when the `state` is used | The cases app's system-assigned identity, with Storage Table Data Contributor on the account (a role that the workflow already gives to a function app) |
| The console | The web pages, served by the same function app from the root address. People open them on the app's own name, or, once the app is closed, on the console's host name on the platform's Application Gateway ([phase 3 for the cases app](07-private-by-default.md#publishing-code-to-the-private-apps-phase-3-for-the-cases-app-the-console-behind-the-platforms-application-gateway-built-not-deployed)). One function takes every route. `/healthz` answers `ok` with no sign-in; every other route is behind the platform's sign-in, and the app refuses (503) when the platform says that sign-in is not on | The platform's sign-in (below); the pages read and write the store with the cases app's system-assigned identity |
| The sign-in | App Service authentication with Microsoft Entra, in front of every route but `/healthz`. The app registration has **no secret and no certificate**: it trusts one user-assigned identity, `id-meternaut-console-auth`, through a federated identity credential, and that identity does nothing else. People must be assigned to the app, and the platform lets in only the object ids of the list of people | `id-meternaut-console-auth` (no Azure role) |
| The people | One setting, a list of the people of the console: object id, name, roles (`owner`, `approver`, `ops`) and the address that letters go to. A person who is not on the list sees nothing, and no letter goes to an address that is not on it | None |
| The approvals | The code that makes a plan, takes an answer and records it. It is the one `Decider` that the pages call | The cases app's system-assigned identity |
| The change, as the approver | The only code of the app that changes Azure, and only with the approving person's own token. `delegated.py` takes an approval in two steps (Approve starts it, `GET /arm/return` finishes it), and `runner.py` makes the change with its own HTTP client and its own list of calls: read and delete of a managed disk and of a public IP address in one resource group, and a read of management locks. Azure checks the person's rights on top of that list. Meternaut holds no right of its own to change Azure | No identity and no role of its own: the approver's access token, for one request, and nothing is stored |
| The timer | `runner-tick`, every minute: closes the plans whose time is over, marks a stuck action (one that never started, or has been running for more than 15 minutes), checks afterwards that the resource is gone and, two days later, that its cost lines stopped, sends the letters about the changes and clears the used tokens once an hour. It cannot change Azure: its list of calls holds three reads | The cases app's system-assigned identity, the identity that only reads |
| The mail | One shared mailbox sends every letter through Microsoft Graph `sendMail`, with the cases app's system-assigned identity. Exchange's role-based access for applications limits that identity to that one mailbox, and the identity holds no Microsoft Entra permission for mail | The same identity, with the Exchange role *Application Mail.Send* limited by a management scope |
| The planted waste | The Hub action `plant` makes an unattached managed disk and, when asked, an unassigned public IP address in the project's resource group, tagged `meternaut-planted`. They are the resources that the loop may touch. A public IP address is optional because the landing zone's policy may deny it | The deployment identity, as for any other resource of the project |

## Who may do what

- A person is known only by the object id that the platform's sign-in puts in a header after it has signed the person in. A form field, a link or a cookie never says who someone is. The app trusts the header only because it refuses to serve anything when the platform's sign-in is off.
- **owner**, **approver** and **ops** are the roles. An approval needs the role *approver* and the person's object id in the plan's list of approvers, which the code takes from the list of people when the plan is made. The change itself needs one more thing that no list gives: the person's own right in Azure to make it. Azure checks that, and Meternaut cannot supply it. The pause page needs *ops*; the permissions page needs *ops* or *owner*. Every person on the list can read the History and manage their own settings, notes and decisions.
- A person cannot approve for somebody else, and cannot approve a plan that is not open, has expired, or has a changed code. A person who signs in to Azure with another account than the one signed in to the console is refused.
- A person can say "not mine": the plan is closed and the case goes back to the people who decide by hand. A person can ask a question: it is recorded, the plan stays open and nothing is approved.

## Safety

- **Nothing of Meternaut's writes to Azure.** The Watch, the console, the mailer and the timer hold no right to change Azure. The test of the app's packaging allows a PUT or a DELETE in the store (Table Storage) and in the change code (Resource Manager) and in no other file; the web package makes no call at all. The change's list holds five calls (read and delete of a disk, read and delete of a public IP address, read of locks), and the timer's list holds the three reads. Azure checks the person's own rights on top of the list.
- **What the change checks again, right before it deletes.** The plan's state, expiry and code and the switches were checked at the press and again at the return from the sign-in, and an approval by a named approver is recorded for the exact plan. Then the code reads the resource again and stops unless the resource is in the one group, subscription and kind that a change may be made on, still exists, is still unattached or unassigned, still has the planted tag, is not marked do-not-touch and has no management lock (read at the resource and at its resource group). A single failed check ends the action as `failed` with the reason and changes nothing. It deletes once; it never retries a delete. It then reads the resource until Azure answers 404 (up to six reads, five seconds apart).
- **There is no delay and no cancel, on purpose.** A delay needs a token that is stored until it ends, and this design stores none. The press of "Approve and sign in to Azure" is the confirmation: the page says that the change runs right after it and cannot be undone.
- **Switches.** `all`, `changes` and `messages` can be pulled on the pause page by a person with the role *ops*. A pulled `changes` switch stops an approval at the press, at the return from the sign-in and right before the delete, so an approval that is in the middle of its sign-in when changes are paused stops; `messages` stops letters. A switch that cannot be read counts as pulled.
- **One approval at a time.** The action of a plan is claimed once, so two approvers who answer at the same moment cannot both record an approval or both delete: the second sees that the plan is being approved at this moment and records nothing. A HEAD request to `/arm/return` is refused, so a prefetch cannot use up a sign-in. The `state` holds the resource that was approved, and the return refuses a plan that names another. The console redirects only to the Microsoft Entra sign-in address, and the change's HTTP client follows no redirect, because it would send the person's token to the new address.
- **The platform may record the code and the `state`.** They are in the URL of `GET /arm/return`, which the platform may record in its request telemetry (not verified). Both work once, and the code is bound to the PKCE verifier (kept on the server) and to the managed identity's client assertion, so a recorded code cannot be redeemed by anybody else. The access token is never logged, stored or shown. The first real run checks it with a query over `requests` for the `/arm/return` URL ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md#security-analysis)).
- **No secret, anywhere.** The sign-in uses a federated credential, and so does the redeem of an approval's code; the mail uses an identity and Exchange's scoped role; the store uses an identity; a change uses the approver's own token, which exists for one request. The test that scans for keys (rule 23) covers the new code and templates, and the app registration is checked by a tool that refuses an app that holds a secret or a certificate.
- **The plan is not a model's.** A model's output is checked against the same schema as before; the plan is built from typed facts, and a test fails if a model's sentence reaches a plan or a letter.
- **A letter says what matters and nothing that is not needed.** The subject starts with `[Meternaut]`, says the point and holds no resource name and no amount of money. The sender's display name ends with "(AI)" and the message carries the header `x-ai-generated: true`. The mailer sends only to an address on the list of people, only as that person on the list, and keeps a record of the kind, subject, time and outcome of each letter, and never its text. Graph's answer 202 means that Graph took the letter, not that it arrived: delivery is proved by receipt.
- **Letters are limited.** A person's weekly budget, a pause that a person set and the choice of the weekly desk hold a letter; an approval letter is the one kind that no budget holds, because a plan that waits for an answer has to reach its approver. A letter that is held or refused is written down with the reason, and a letter that was held or failed is offered again by the timer after 15 minutes, at most 12 times, because Exchange can take up to two hours to apply a new right to send. A letter that was sent is never sent again.
- **Forms.** A form is accepted only as a POST from the same site, in the form content type, at most 16 KB, with a one-use token that belongs to the person and the plan. Every page carries a strict content security policy and no script; a page never shows a trace, and a fault says only that nothing was changed. The policy's `form-action` allows the console and `https://login.microsoftonline.com`: the approve form is answered with a redirect to the sign-in, and Chromium-based browsers check that redirect against `form-action` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/HTTP/Reference/Headers/Content-Security-Policy/form-action) says that browsers differ).
- **The loop is optional.** If the store's name is not set, the app is the Watch alone, as it was before. If it is set, every setting that the loop needs has to be valid, or the loop does not start. The settings of the sign-in to Azure are set together or not at all: without them the loop works and nobody can approve a change, and the page says so.

## What a person has to do once

A person who owns the place does each of these once, with their own sign-in. Each has a tool that reads before it writes, changes nothing without `--apply`, refuses to run in a pipeline and removes nothing. The commands are in [the bootstrap page](../../environment/BOOTSTRAP.md#added-on-2026-11-05-for-the-console-and-the-approval-loop).

| What | Tool | Who |
|---|---|---|
| The app registration for the sign-in, with no secret: the one delegated permission to use Azure as the signed-in person (Azure Service Management `user_impersonation`) with an administrator's consent where the tenant requires it, the redirect address of the approval's return (`/arm/return`), and the people who may sign in | `tools/entra-setup.py` | A person who may administer Microsoft Entra |
| The shared mailbox and the one scoped right to send as it | `tools/exchange-setup.py` | A person who may administer Exchange Online |
| The repository variables `CONSOLE_APP_CLIENT_ID`, `CONSOLE_PEOPLE` and `MAIL_SENDER` | `gh variable set` | The owner of the repository |

No step grants Meternaut a right to change anything: the person who approves needs their own right in Azure to make the change ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md#what-changes-for-a-customer)). After the first run of the action `cases`, which makes the identity of the sign-in, and these steps, the action `cases` is run again so that the platform's sign-in is switched on; the action `loop-check` then reads the front door from outside and fails if anything but `/healthz` can be read without signing in, or if a forged sign-in header opens a page.

## Price

About zero a month more, on top of the 13 US dollars a month that the cases app's Basic plan costs (see [the first cases](02-first-cases.md#price)). The tables are in the existing storage account, and a few hundred small records cost cents. The timer runs a function every minute on that always-on plan, which has room for it. Mail through Graph from a shared mailbox without a licence costs nothing (a shared mailbox that is not over 50 GB and is not used to sign in needs no licence, [Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/exchange/recipients-in-exchange-online/manage-user-mailboxes/shared-mailboxes)). The planted disk (4 GiB, Standard SSD) costs less than a euro a month until it is deleted, which is the point; the optional public IP address costs about three euros a month until it is deleted.

## Known limits

Written down so that nobody has to find them.

- **The change is made in the same app as the Watch.** It uses its own HTTP client with its own list of calls, and it holds the person's token for one request, but it runs in the same process and the same deployment as the Watch and the console. A flaw that gives an attacker code execution in the app during an approval could use that token for whatever the approver may do at Azure, until the token expires. A separate app for the console and the change would shrink that exposure ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md#security-analysis)).
- **Nobody can cancel after the press.** There is no delay and no cancel button: the press of "Approve and sign in to Azure" is the confirmation. A second approver who is told about a plan cannot stop the first one's approval, and there is no rule yet that two people must approve.
- **When Azure refuses the change** (403, or a claims challenge that asks for another multifactor sign-in), the plan closes as failed for every approver and a new plan follows on a later run. There is no step-up flow with the `claims` parameter yet. If the store fails between the claim on the plan's action and the recorded decision, the console releases the claim on a best-effort basis ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md#known-limits)).
- **Quiet hours are not applied.** A letter can arrive at any hour. The settings page holds the choice, and the mailer does not read it yet.
- **Approvals are not counted against the weekly budget of letters,** by design, so a plan always reaches its approver; a person who wants fewer of them can pause messages, and then the plan expires.
- **Teams is not built.** The person is told by letter only. A Teams card needs an app that a guest of the tenant may not be able to install; it is untested. A card could not carry the sign-in to Azure either: it would open the plan page.
- **The mail is accepted, not proved to be delivered.** Graph's 202 is not receipt. The first delivery to a person's inbox in another organisation is the proof, and a mail-flow rule that rejects mail from the mailbox to anybody who is not on the list of people is not made by any tool here.
- **Exchange needs time.** A new role assignment or a changed scope applies to live sends after 30 minutes to two hours.
- **The saving of a new resource is unknown at first.** A resource that was made a moment ago has no cost lines, so the plan shows no saving and says so; the check two days later reads the cost lines.
- **Only two kinds of resource, and only what fits in a request.** The change code cannot delete anything else, and it cannot resize, stop or buy anything. A request waits for a disk or a public IP address, which Azure deletes in seconds. A change that takes minutes, a change on a schedule and a purchase each need a design of their own ([run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md#known-limits)).
- **The History workbook still depends on the viewer's network.** The console shows the same runs and cases from the store.
- **The sign-in to Azure is proved once on the real estate** (2026-11-05: one approver who had the right, the consent given beforehand, no Conditional Access refusal). A refusal by Azure, a sign-in that needs consent, a Conditional Access refusal and a claims challenge for another multifactor check have not been seen there; the first of these is tested with fakes only.
- **A form sent after the sign-in has expired is untested.** The platform then answers the POST with a redirect to Microsoft Entra. The page's `form-action` now allows the sign-in address of Microsoft Entra, so a Chromium browser should follow that redirect. This has not been tried. Reloading the page starts a new sign-in, because a GET without a session is sent to Entra.

## Not built, and next

A Teams card for the approval (it would open the plan page, because a card cannot carry the sign-in to Azure; and only after a test of whether a guest of the tenant can receive it), the approval by a second person for a larger change (who makes the change, and with whose rights, is not decided), the agents' move into the Foundry Agent Service as hosted agents, a separate app for the console and the change, quiet hours, an alert when a timer round fails, and more families of change. A family has to fit [run as the approver](05-run-as-the-approver.md): a plan that code writes, a person's answer, the person's own sign-in to Azure, a re-check right before the change, and a check afterwards. A change that takes longer than a request, such as a resize of a virtual machine, needs a design of its own. A change on a schedule is not possible. A purchase is advice that a person carries out with their own right to buy. A customer's own pipeline is the option that fits an estate that is managed as code.
