# Beats: the investigation looks at the estate in parts, by what it costs — a decision record

**Status: decided on 2026-11-17, built and tested against fakes and scripted Foundry runs, and run three times on a real estate and a real model: the sections on the real runs say what each showed and what the round after it changed. The arithmetic below is a design that the real runs score.** Until the function app that carries it is published, nothing changes. Nothing was changed in Azure for this record.

## What was asked, and the evidence

One Investigator session looks at the estate as a single conversation, and it starves. The real runs of 2026-11-16 and 2026-11-17 on ten subscriptions (7,920 US dollars in 30 days) made 22 to 29 tool calls in 414 to 600 seconds and used 340,000 to 360,000 tokens, and the agent's own notes for the dearest kinds read "not examined before tool budget ended", "measurement hit budget limit" and "stopped because tool budget was exhausted". The first improved run found one quiet service and said of a large network appliance only that it had data processed and no waste was found: it looked for idle, and never asked whether the thing is larger than its use needs ([proposal 12](12-investigation-toolbox.md)). Which kinds got looked at depended on what the agent happened to open first.

The cost of a session is the second fact. A model reads the whole conversation again at every step, so the tokens of a session grow with the square of its steps: 22 calls were 345,323 tokens through the gateway, about 1.4 to 4 US dollars for each million tokens (the price through the gateway lies between the two), so about 0.5 to 1.4 dollars for one session. The owner's standing limit for each caller is 5 US dollars a day and 25 a month ([rule 15 (g)](../../AGENTS.md)), 50,000 tokens a minute and 60 requests a minute. Several full sessions in a Watch do not fit; a few short ones, started from what code has read already, do.

## The decision

1. **Code chooses the parts, by dollars, and names no kind.** From the cost lines that the Watch has already read (the same data as `estate_overview`), [`cases/beats.py`](../../src/case-functions/cases/beats.py) ranks the kinds of resource, and the lines of the bill that no resource owns as one more entry, by their cost of the last 30 days, dearest first. It keeps the dearest entries that together make 95 percent of the cost (`FLOOR_PERCENT`). A beat opens with the dearest entry that no beat has and takes the dearer-first entries of the same service after it, two kinds at most (`BEAT_KINDS`, three until the third real run: the dearest items get more of a session; the service is the part of the type before the slash). The last beat holds what is left: the kinds below the floor and those that do not fit in the number of beats, and it names the dearest eight (`CATCH_ALL_KINDS`) and counts the others. The plan has at most `INVESTIGATE_BEATS` beats (8, six until the third real run), is the same for the same bill whatever the order of its lines, and is never made of a word that the code wrote: a kind goes in as the word that the cost lines gave and comes out as the same word. A test fails when a kind of resource is written in the module.
2. **One session of the Investigator for each beat, one after the other, on one toolbox.** The Foundry client is not safe for a second thread ([proposal 12](12-investigation-toolbox.md)). A beat has its own budget of tool calls, seconds and tokens, and every beat shares the run's toolbox: its handles, its evidence, its caches, its register of meters and its pass over the references, so that nothing is read twice. The toolbox allows 40 calls and what a run adds; each beat raises that when it starts to the calls made so far plus its own allowance, and never lowers it, so that a beat that starts late is not starved by the ones before it.
3. **Code reads the pictures first.** Before a beat starts, code calls the tools that the agent has, through the same toolbox, with no model: the listing of each of the beat's first five kinds, dearest first, and then two calls of `profile` for the five dearest resources (the dearest of each kind first, then the next dearest of each; since 2026-11-19 the dearest that no run of the last 21 days looked at, [below](#each-run-looks-at-resources-that-the-earlier-runs-did-not-2026-11-19)): **the dearest three at the grain of the hour and the other two at the day**. The beat's input holds the two answers, so that the first step already has the facts, the meters, the metrics with data, the links and the age of the resources that cost most. The alternative was to leave it to the agent's own `profile` calls: that spends the first two or three steps of the beat on listing and reading, about a quarter of its tokens by the arithmetic below, to arrive where this one starts. What a tool answers is typed values and handles, as it always was, so nothing of the estate's text reaches a model, and the cited numbers are checked against the same evidence. **Why the hour for three.** A row of a metric at the grain of the day has the total, the maximum and the average of daily buckets and no peak; what a resource must carry, which is what decides whether it is larger than it needs to be, shows in the peak and the 95th percentile of the finer buckets, and the picture gives those only at the hour. The orchestrator measured on the real estate (2026-11-17, read only, no model, ten subscriptions, `main` at `9a4720b`) the picture of the dearest resource of each of the five dearest kinds: **24 seconds at the day and 39.5 at the hour**, both complete (`partial` false, nothing unread), and each filling the 16,000 characters that the answer may have (15,995 and 15,988). Three at the hour is one for each kind of a beat that holds three, and the three dearest of a beat of one kind; the other two cost less at the day. The pair was estimated at about 34 seconds from those two figures (8 seconds a resource at the hour, 5 at the day); the whole seeding was then timed on the real estate, beat by beat, and is in the next section ('What the seeds took'). **Each of the two pictures that code reads has a time of its own, `SEED_PICTURE_SECONDS` (25 seconds), inside the 45 that the reads of a call may take:** what is not read by then is `unread` in the answer, as it is for any call whose time runs out, and the Investigator's own `profile` calls keep the seconds of the toolbox. It is a bound by time, and the count of the metrics bounds the hour too: **`dossier.HOURLY_METRICS` (20)**, the number of names that one request of Azure Monitor takes (Learn, "Metrics - List", as `cases/arm.py` cites it). A picture by the hour of a resource that offers more than 20 metrics reads all of them at the day first, and then the 20 with the greatest daily maximum at the hour (ties by name): the hour costs one request of Azure Monitor whatever the resource offers, where it cost one for each 20 metrics, and the answer says `at_hour` (how many it read at the hour) beside `offered` and `with_data` (which counts all that have data); the evidence holds the rows that were shown and no others. A resource that offers 20 or fewer is read as before, with no day read. The ranking is by the raw maximum, which is a bound on the cost and no judgement of importance (units are not comparable, so a count outranks a percentage); the Investigator can read any other metric with `metric_summary`. On the fakes, a resource of 45 metrics (40 with data) takes three requests at the day and one at the hour of 20 names, where it took three at the hour; one of 99 would take five at the day and one at the hour, where it took five at the hour, which are the heavy ones (a month of hourly buckets for 20 metrics in each). What that saves in seconds on the real estate is not measured: a day request is lighter than an hour request, but it is not free, and the time of 25 seconds for a picture still bounds the worst case. The Investigator can still ask for an hourly picture of any other resource. **After the pictures, code also reads the list prices of the dearest meter of the dearest resource, and the input holds them** ([below](#the-fifth-and-sixth-real-runs-and-the-round-after-them-the-list-prices-2026-11-18)).
4. **A beat is told what the beats before it concluded, as typed lines.** For each earlier beat: its kinds and, when it gave an answer, the basis, the proposal and the handles of its findings and its own `examined` phrases (cleaned like every text of an AI; at most 3,000 characters in all, the oldest phrases cut first); when it gave none, only that it did not conclude, so that the next one knows its kinds were not judged. The instructions say in one general sentence what a beat is, that it may leave its part only when the links of a resource lead elsewhere, and that the lines of the bill that no resource owns can be a beat of their own. No kind, measurement or rule of waste is in the sentence, and the test that scans the instructions for such words holds.
5. **One pool of findings.** Findings of all beats are numbered in turn and pooled. A resource is in one finding only, and **the surer finding keeps it**: a later beat's finding about a resource that an earlier finding has takes it only when its confidence is higher (the earlier finding gives it up, which is counted as `surpassed` on its line of the History, and it is dropped with that reason when none is left); with the same confidence the earlier keeps it, and the later finding loses it, which is counted as `repeated`, and is dropped with that reason when none is left. A finding that the check of the cited measurements drops (a handle that no tool showed, a claim of no usage that no measurement proves) never takes a resource, so a resource does not end with no finding because a finding that could not stand had it first. What the guards, the check of 'judged lately' and the cap of cases do afterwards is about the resource (the guards) or the facts that a finding cites ('judged lately'), and is done after the beats, on the findings that are left: a resource that is held back there is held back for the finding that has it, and it is not handed to the finding that lost it, which would meet the same guards, but which might cite other facts than the first for 'judged lately'; that is a rare case that this record does not cover. Every finding goes through `_verify`, the guards, the pricing of [proposal 24](24-priced-findings.md) and the rules of the cases exactly as a finding of one session does; the cap of cases for a run and the limit of open requests are what they were.
6. **The Skeptic judges all the survivors, in as many calls as they take.** A finding joins the group before it while every resource of the group keeps the rows of the second richest level of detail within its input of 30,000 characters, so that it is not given poorer rows for being given more findings. At most four calls (`SKEPTIC_CALLS`), each with its own record in the harness; a finding that gets none has no verdict and goes to a person, as a finding without a verdict always did. The input is built by code in one place, so that the next change, which gives the Skeptic tools, can take the seam.
7. **A beat that fails is a note; a brake stops all of them.** A refusal of the model service, a time overrun, an answer that cannot be read or **a beat that uses more tokens than its hard limit** (twice its cap, or its cap and 60,000, whichever is more: `harness.beat_limit`; what a beat can come to is that and two steps more, `harness.beat_bound`, 250,000 at the cap of 100,000, see the budgets) fails that beat only: a note ("over its limit of …", and the others go on), `ok` false on its line `investigation.beat`, `failed` with the reason `tokens` on its line `harness.run`, and the other beats still count and keep their findings; **it pulls no switch and sends no letter**, because a beat that asks once more after its cap is a model that was slow to stop and no loop. When no beat gave an answer, the investigation failed, as one session did. A refusal of the harness (a pulled switch, a stored definition that differs, three refusals in one run, a repeated call) in any beat holds the whole investigation: no case is opened from findings that nobody judged, and a run with no cap (the open beat) keeps its brake on tokens, which pulls the switch. The harness gives each beat a record of its own, so the three refusals count in one beat and its trace line holds that beat's tokens.

`INVESTIGATE_BEATS=1`, and an estate whose cost could not be read, give one open beat: the whole estate as one session, with `INVESTIGATE_MAX_CALLS` calls and **480 seconds for tool calls (`OPEN_SECONDS`), as one session had them when the investigation had 600 seconds, whatever the seconds of the investigation are**. That is the way back, and what every older test exercises.

## The arithmetic

Measured on the fakes, with the real toolbox and an invented estate of five resources of one kind with seven metrics each: the reads that code makes for a beat are one listing for each of its kinds (224 to 282 characters each on the fakes) and the two pictures, and **the beat's first input is 7,057 characters**: the pictures of 4,244 (three at the hour) and 2,666 (two at the day) and what else the input holds. On the fakes one picture of five at the day is 6,582 characters and at the hour 7,038. A picture of the real estate fills its cap, and the two caps are 9,600 for three handles and 6,400 for two, 16,000 in all, the same as the cap of one picture of five: the first input is then at most about 16,300 characters, and about 19,300 with the 3,000 that the earlier beats may add, about 4,800 tokens at four characters a token. Computed from the code, on the tip after the merge of `main` at `790936d`: every step of every session also carries the instructions (10,064 characters) and the definitions of the tools (27,058 characters for the 23 that the toolbox lists), about 37,000 characters, which is about 9,300 tokens at four characters a token (an estimate; the real count is not measured here). So a beat of six steps costs about six times (9,300 plus 1,300 to 4,000 for the pictures) plus what its tool answers add to every later step, about 87,000 to 102,000 tokens: the target is six steps and 100,000 tokens, which is what the soft cap holds. After the cap the agent has the answers of its refused calls and writes its answer in one or two rounds, so a beat ends between 100,000 and 140,000 tokens.

**What the Investigator's own `profile` of the same resources reads again.** The two calls of the seed are calls of the tool `profile` with the arguments that the Investigator could give itself (`handles`, `days` null, `grain` hour or day), and the toolbox keeps what they read. A test that counts the questions that the fake Azure got shows that a second call for the same handles, at the same or at the other grain, asks Resource Graph, Cost Management, Advisor and the Activity Log nothing: the references, the ages, the Advisor items, the meters, the Activity Log and the diagnostic settings are kept. **The metrics are not kept**: the second call reads them again (33 reads for three handles at the hour on the fake, against 53 for the whole seed). So a repeat is not a second read of 25 to 40 seconds and is not free; what the metrics take of the 24 and 39.5 seconds on the real estate is not known, as those figures are for all the parts. A cache of the metric reads was not made here: the first real run showed that the Investigator does repeat a picture (5 calls, about 120 seconds), and the round after it keeps a picture that was read in full and answers its repeat at once (below).

**What the seeds took on the real estate.** The orchestrator rehearsed the plan and the seeds of tip `8cd40b2` on the real estate (read only, no model, ten subscriptions, 2026-11-17): the plan of the real costs, then the seed of each beat through `Toolbox.preload`. The plan has 22 kinds with a cost: the first beat has three kinds that are about a third of the dollars, the next four beats one kind each, the sixth is the catch-all that names eight kinds, and about seven smaller kinds are in no beat and only counted, which is right for a first run. **The seeds took 56.0, 31.9, 104.1, 20.9, 7.6 and 29.2 seconds: 250 seconds in all, 24 tool calls, none failed.** A picture is 7 to 10.5 thousand characters at the hour and 4 to 7 at the day, so the input of a beat is 12 to 17 thousand characters (on the fakes it is 7,057). The third seed took 104 seconds because the three resources of its kind, read at the hour, offer about 99 metrics each; while a beat's 160 seconds included its reads, the model had about 56 seconds there and about 100 in the first beat. **So a beat has a time for the model that does not depend on its reads**: the seconds of the setting (`INVESTIGATE_BEAT_SECONDS`) for the model and its tool calls, on top of the reads, inside the time of the investigation; a beat whose model time is left under `STEP_SECONDS` (30) is not asked at all and fails at no cost in tokens; and the two pictures of a seed have 25 seconds each and read at most 20 metrics of a resource at the hour, so the third seed reads one hourly request for each of its resources where it read five, and cannot take much more than its listings (7 to 10 seconds), the day reads and two pictures: about 60 seconds. That bound is by construction (a test sees the 25 seconds on the code's calls and the toolbox's own on the agent's, and another counts the requests of a resource of 45 metrics), the rehearsal was made before it, and only a real run shows the seconds.

**The arithmetic of the seconds (after the first real run, for eight beats).** A beat has its reads (about 42 seconds on average: 250 for six, measured), then at most 110 seconds for the model and its tool calls (half of the 220), and a minute for its answer: **at most 42 + 170 = 212 seconds, 237 with the last request that a cut-off beat is given (25 seconds)**. Typically it is less: three tool calls of about 18 seconds and six steps of about 8 seconds are about 100 seconds, and the answer 8 more, so about 150 with the reads, and the third real run measured 135 a beat (807 seconds for six). **Eight beats at the typical 140 are 1,120 seconds, and with the 120 for the checks and the Skeptic 1,240, inside the 1,440: the typical Watch has about 200 seconds to spare.** At the worst a beat takes 237 seconds, so five of them take 1,185 of the 1,320 that the beats have (1,440 less the 120), and the start rule (reads plus 170, about 212) does not start a sixth: the worst Watch looks at five parts, as six beats did, and **the kinds of the others, the cheapest, are named in the record as not looked at, which is acceptable for a first run and is the price of giving the others the time that they asked for**. Depth costs nothing at the worst and gives the typical Watch two parts more. The whole Watch: reading 345 seconds (up to 540), the investigation (about 1,240 typically with eight beats, 1,440 at the most), the cases and the series (about 3 minutes) and the telemetry that arrives late (about 3 minutes): **about 32 minutes typically (29 with six beats) and 36 at the most, against the 38 that `cases-run` waits; with the slowest reading (540 seconds) and all of the 1,440 it is about 39, one minute more than the wait, and the check would say that no end of the run arrived in 38 minutes although the Watch ends inside the function's 45.** That case is not covered here (the workflow is the owner's). Depth does not change it, because the seconds of the investigation stay 1,440, but it makes it more likely, because the typical investigation is 1,240 seconds where it was 1,000: how often an investigation reaches its 1,440 is what the first runs of eight beats show. The third real run took 1,003 seconds in all, 807 of them the investigation: its reading, cases and series took 196 seconds, less than the 345 and about 180 that this arithmetic takes.

## The first real run of the beats, and what the round after it changes (2026-11-17)

**What it showed** (counts of the History only): 6 beats in 848 seconds, 26 tool calls of the Investigator and 545,708 tokens. Five of the six beats **failed**, four by their hard time and one by its tokens, and what they had found was lost: beat 1 took 128 seconds, 4 calls and 69 thousand tokens and ended by its time; beat 2 136 seconds, 2 calls, 46 thousand, time; beat 3 118 seconds, 5 calls, 122 thousand tokens, time, and it was the one that gave its answer (one finding); beat 4 151 seconds, 5 calls, 105 thousand, time; beat 5 133 seconds, 4 calls, 91 thousand, time; beat 6 149 seconds, 6 calls, 110 thousand, tokens. Of the 848 seconds only 303 were the model's (38 steps of about 8 seconds); the tools took 405: `profile` 17 calls and 303 seconds (about 18 each: the 12 pictures that code read took about 220 of them, and the model asked for 5 more itself, at 20 to 28 seconds each, of resources that the pictures already held) and `cost_meters` one call and 63 seconds. The gateway answered "not now" 10 times: 545 thousand tokens in 848 seconds is 38.6 thousand a minute against the 50 thousand that are allowed.

**Why.** The model's time in a beat was `min(setting, 100)`: raising the setting to 220 would have changed nothing. Its tool calls were refused after 70 percent of 100 seconds, which came before the reads of 20 seconds that it had asked for, and the hard time was the 100 seconds, with a call in flight and the steps of an answer (8 seconds each) still to come. A beat that fails keeps nothing, so a few seconds late cost the whole beat.

**What this round changes, all of it generic (no kind of resource, rule 30):**

1. **More time.** The seconds of a beat are the setting, 220 (ceiling 300), and the seconds of the investigation 1,440 (ceiling 1,500; the template, `config.py`, the Bicep and the pins moved together). The arithmetic is above.
2. **An early answer.** The tool calls of a beat are refused after 3 (`INVESTIGATE_BEAT_CALLS`) or after half of its seconds, whichever comes first (it was 8 calls, and 70 percent), and its hard time is that and 60 seconds more (`beats.times`), so that the steps of an answer always fit. The open session of the way back keeps 70 percent.
3. **A last request.** A run that is cut off (its hard time, its tokens, or the three rounds that it was given to write its text and gave none) is asked **once more, with no tool and 25 seconds**, to write its answer now (`Foundry.run`, `salvage`), when at least 40 seconds are left in the time of the beats. The request carries the answers that were waiting and one instruction; if the agent asks for a tool again, or the request fails, the beat fails as it did. What the answer holds counts like any other (the checks of code are the same), the line of the beat in the History keeps its `end` (`time`, `tokens`) and has `salvaged: true`, and a beat that did this is no failed beat and no trip: it is an answer that cost more than its cap (the line `harness.run` says `ok`, reason `salvaged`). A brake that is not the tokens of a beat (a pulled switch, three refusals) still ends the run first. The request re-reads the conversation, so it costs one more step: about 13 to 25 thousand tokens, up to about 60 thousand for a long one, which the start rule of 60 thousand tokens already allows for.
4. **One sentence** in the instructions: *The pictures that you are given already hold what a call would read again: judge them first, ask for more only for a resource or a fact that they lack, and write your answer as soon as you can say what you found.* It names no kind and no rule, and the digests of the lock moved with it (the definition of the Investigator, and the toolbox and dossier modules for the next point).
5. **A plain repeat of a picture is free.** `profile` keeps the parts of a picture that were read in full (no section unread or unavailable, no failed metric or meter, the references read to their end) by handle, days and grain. A call for handles that are all kept at the same days and grain is answered from them, with no request to Azure, and its line in the History has `savedSeconds`, the seconds that those reads took. A repeat used to read the metrics again, and they are what takes the time (the 5 repeats of the first run were about 120 seconds). Another grain, other days or a part that was not read in full are read as before.
6. **Tokens do not change**: 100,000 for a beat, 800,000 in all. With fewer calls in a beat the tokens of a beat should fall (they grow with the square of the steps), and the gateway's limit of a minute is nearly binding at 38.6 thousand a minute: the next real run will show whether the 429s go.

**Not proved.** That a beat now gives its answer in time (the first run after this is the test), that the last request is accepted by the service as written (the Responses interface takes a message and the outputs of the calls that wait together; it was written from the interface and tested against the fake, not against the service), and the seconds that the kept pictures save.

- **A month** (25): the weekly Watch and the weekly Study, 4.35 times, are 19.6 at the dearer price and 8.3 at the cheaper one at the worst size (4.0 plus 0.5, and 1.4 plus 0.5) and 12.2 and 4.4 at the typical size (2.3 plus 0.5, and 0.8 plus 0.2), so two runs that a person starts, of the typical size, fit in the rest at the worst (5.4 left at the dearer price). A beat of 100,000 tokens needs 2 minutes at that rate and a beat has 110 seconds for the model and its tool calls and a minute for its answer, so a beat that spends its whole cap is held back by the gateway or ends by its time before it ends by its tokens. When the gateway answers 429 the client waits (at most 90 seconds a call); a beat that is slowed down has its tool calls refused after half of its time and still writes its answer, and a beat that is cut off by its hard time or its tokens is asked once more for its answer and is a failed beat, which loses its findings, only when that fails too. The first real run showed 10 such waits at 38.6 thousand tokens a minute.
- **Time.** Reading ten subscriptions took 345 seconds. A Watch of that size is 345 reading, about 1,240 investigating with eight beats (1,440 at the most), a few minutes for the cases and the series, and the telemetry arrives late: **about 32 minutes typically and 36 at the most, and about 39 when the reading takes its 9 minutes and the investigation all of its 1,440 seconds**, against **the 38 minutes that the action `cases-run` waits (`--minutes 38`) and the 45 minutes (2,700 seconds) that its container group is given**, both already on `main` (pull request 51); the arithmetic is above. ## The third real run, and the round after it: depth, why a claim fails, a floor, the notes (2026-11-17)

Scored outside the app against a private list of known waste that is not in this repository (a check of the results, never an input: [rule 30](../../AGENTS.md)), the four runs touched 4, 5, 3 and 7 of its 22 candidates, always the dearest few, and 15 had been touched by no run. A fifth run would have taught nothing that the fourth had not. The open questions that the Investigator recalls keep the same resources in play until a person decides them, and that stays right. What was missing is the other half: a memory of what was looked at.

**What it does.** Code only. No instruction, tool description or tool schema changed, so the agents in Foundry are as they were; the lock moved by the one digest of the module of the tools, and by nothing that an agent is defined by.

1. **The record remembers.** `InvestigationRecord.profiled` holds, for each resource of which a call of `profile` read some of the picture, the first 12 hex characters of the SHA-256 of its lower-case id: the pictures that code reads for the seed and those that the Investigator asks for. Never the id, in the record, the History, a note or a page. It is written for every run that was tried, also one that ended early or failed, and a record from before reads as nothing looked at. A picture of which every part was unread is no look. A picture that was read in part is one, because a resource that is too heavy to be read in full in its time would else stay at the front for ever.
2. **The seeds move on.** At its start the investigation reads the records of the last 21 days (`LOOKED_DAYS`, by the `at` of each) and gives the toolbox their hashes (`Toolbox.window`). The listing that code makes for a seed (`list_resources` by cost, called by `beats.seed` through `recorded(beat, "code", n)`) then puts the resources that neither the window nor this run has looked at first, each group by cost and then by key (`Toolbox.rotate`, which `recorded` sets around that one call, as it sets `trial`). When every resource of a kind was looked at it is the dearest first, so a beat never turns empty because of this, and with no memory it is the order of before, except that a resource whose picture an earlier beat of the same run asked for comes last (intended, because nobody needs the picture of one resource twice in a run; a test pins it with the two beats of a first run). When the records cannot be read, the seeds are the dearest first, as before, and the run says so in its first note, "The earlier investigations could not be read, so this run took the dearest resources first.", and claims nothing of what is new. **The runs after the ones from before the hashes** would have a memory that holds only their own hashes, and would seed the five that the old runs had already seeded. So, for each kind that a record without a hash touched (a record keeps the `kinds` that its tools touched), the dearest `SEED_RESOURCES` (five) resources of the kind, in the same cost order, count as looked at for the reads of code, for as long as such a record is in the window (a count for each of those kinds, `Toolbox.backfill`, which the investigation sets; no id and no hash of what is only assumed is kept; the Investigator's own listing is not touched). A kind that no such record touched has nothing assumed, and its dearest five are seeded as they were. A record without a hash that touched no kind, such as one of a run that failed early, assumes nothing. The hashes of the records that have them count on top of that, always. So the first run after the change seeds the next five of those kinds, the second run the third five, and none of them the first five again. The assumption lasts 21 days after the last run that was not recorded by resource, and after that only the hashes rule. A record that a new run wrote with a kind and no hash (the run could read no picture of it) cannot be told from one of the old runs and counts the same: the dearest five of that kind count as seen for 21 days after it, though nobody looked at them. `lookedAt` counts the assumed and the real resources of the beat's kinds together, none twice. The note counts nothing as new and says why: "Some of the earlier runs were not recorded by resource, so the dearest resources of the kinds that they looked at were counted as seen." With no record at all the first run is in the old order and "for the first time" is true. **This is an assumption about what the earlier runs looked at, and not a record of it** (see below). **The Investigator's own call of `list_resources` with the order cost still means the dearest first**: the switch is off for it, a test shows that the same call gives the same text whatever the memory holds, and another that only the read of code turns the switch on.
3. **The last beat** names the kinds that no run of the window touched (the kinds of the records) first and then the dearest, so that over the runs every kind has its turn, and counts the others as before. A record keeps 20 kinds at most, dearest first, so the cheapest kinds that a long run touched can look untouched and get one more turn: it errs towards looking again, never towards skipping.
4. **The beat is told**, in one typed field, `lookedAt` (`days` and `resources`), how many of the resources of its kinds the runs of the last 21 days looked at, so that it does not take the pictures for the dearest five. It is left out when there are none, so the input of a first run is the one of before.
5. **What people read.** One note, the first of the notes of the run (a run keeps its first 20): "This run looked at 19 resources, 14 of them for the first time in 21 days. In 21 days Meternaut has looked at 52 resources." It reads right at none and at one resource, and has one sentence when the window holds nothing more. `investigation.finished` carries the counts `profiled`, `firstTime` and `inWindow`, and nothing else of a resource.

**How many runs are kept.** `KEPT_INVESTIGATIONS` is 20. The timer investigates every 7 days (`INVESTIGATE_EVERY_DAYS`), so 20 records hold 140 days and the window of 21 is in them. A person can start runs too: with more than 20 runs in 21 days the window is the 20 newest, which still moves the seeds on. It is not raised: the page "What the AI looked at" reads that many records and the number is pinned. A record holds at most 300 hashes (about 4,500 of the 30,000 characters of a row of the store), and a run at today's budgets reads about 160 pictures at the most.

**What it costs.** About 1,000 bytes more in the archive of the sources that went to the Operator then (the median of several builds: one build differed by about 60 bytes; [proposal 27](27-package-without-comments.md) says why nothing measures that archive now), and one read of the table of the records at the start of a run.

**Not proved.** That rotation finds more waste for each run on the real estate: coverage of resources is not coverage of waste, and the next real run is scored against the private list. That 21 days is the right window: it is a constant, not an estimate of how fast an estate changes, and a resource that was looked at and then changed is not looked at again before the window ends. That a resource is remembered only when `profile` read it: one that the Investigator described or measured with another tool is not, and may be seeded again. That a run which is cut off inside its investigation is remembered: it writes no record ([below](#a-watch-is-never-run-again-by-a-redelivery)), so what it looked at is looked at again. That the beat uses what `lookedAt` tells it: no model was called. **That the backfill is what the earlier runs looked at.** It is an assumption about what the earlier runs looked at, and not a record of it, and it lasts 21 days after the last run that was not recorded by resource. It holds for the kinds that a record without a hash touched, and a kind that the old runs only listed, or touched with a tool that shows no picture, counts the same as one whose pictures they read, so its dearest five are taken as seen though nobody looked at them. A beat of two kinds profiled five resources in all and not five of each, so it counts more as seen than was. The costs may have moved since, so the dearest five today may not be the five that were seeded. A run of the new code that touched a kind and read no picture of it leaves a record that cannot be told from an old one. A record keeps 20 kinds at most, so the cheapest kinds that a long run touched are not assumed, and are seeded from the dearest, as before.

## A Watch is never run again by a redelivery

The control queue hands a message over again when the invocation that had it was cut off (a recycled worker, a platform update with its ten minutes of grace, the function's time) and `maxDequeueCount` is 5. Until now only a message older than 15 minutes was refused, so a Watch cut off in its first minutes and handed over again within 15 minutes of the message would have begun a second paid run; a Watch of 30 minutes makes such a cut more likely. `control.handle_message` now refuses, and records as `run.failed`, a message for a Watch or a Study that the queue says it has handed over before (`dequeue_count` above 1), whatever its age; the card test, which makes no model call, is not held. The owner starts a new run by hand. What a run that is cut off leaves behind (a case opened, a letter sent) is what it always was: a case is keyed by its resource and a run that is repeated by hand finds it judged lately.

**The wait of 7 days starts from a Watch that spent tokens, whatever came of it.** `investigation_wait` counts from the runs that investigated, and that was true only when a beat gave an answer: a Watch in which every beat failed (a hard time, answers that could not be read) spent up to about 1,000,000 tokens (the worst row of the table above) and started no wait, so the daily timer would have run it again the next day, against the 5 US dollars of a day and 25 of a month. A run now counts as having investigated when a beat answered **or any token was spent**; a run that could not begin (nothing read, no model call) still starts no wait. The catch-up of the timer after a worker restart (a timer that is past due and fires again 10 or 50 minutes later) is tested with the fake clock: it starts no second Watch inside the wait. **What this does not cover:** a Watch that is cut off inside its investigation writes no record of the run, so there is nothing to count from, and a timer that is past due after the restart could start one more Watch; the guard above holds the redelivery of the queue and not the timer. It is rare (the function's time is 45 minutes and a Watch ends at about 33 at the slowest), it is bounded by the limits of tokens and dollars of a day and a month, and starting the wait earlier would undo the rule that a run that could not investigate starts none.

## What is recorded and shown

- **History.** `investigation.beat`, one line for each beat: `beat`, `of`, `kinds` (how many), `billing`, `seeded` (the pictures that code read), `looked` (how many of its kinds its tools read), `toolCalls`, `tokens`, `seconds`, `end` (`answered`, `time`, `calls`, `tokens`, `refused`, `empty` or `failed`), `ok`, `findings` and, only when true, `salvaged` (the beat was cut off and gave its answer when it was asked once more). `investigation.call`, one line for each tool call ([below](#one-line-for-each-tool-call)). `investigation.started` and `investigation.finished` say how many beats there are (`beats`) and carry the totals, and `investigation.finished` the counts of what the run looked at ([above](#each-run-looks-at-resources-that-the-earlier-runs-did-not-2026-11-19)); `investigation.finding` says which beat wrote it. The tool that reads a run for the Hub (`tools/cases-check.py`) takes the first start and the first end, and counts an end with `ok` false as a failed investigation, which a failed beat must not be: that is why a beat has a line of its own and no pair of its own.
- **The record of the investigation** ([proposal 23](23-what-the-ai-looked-at.md)) gains one line for each beat (its kinds, calls, seconds and tokens, how it ended, how many of its kinds its tools read and the first three phrases of what it said that it examined) and `more`, the findings that the record had no room for (it keeps at most 16 when it has beats, and fewer when a row of the store would not take them), and, since 2026-11-19, `profiled`, the hashes of the resources whose picture was read ([above](#each-run-looks-at-resources-that-the-earlier-runs-did-not-2026-11-19)). No amount of money and no share of the bill is in it, nor on the page: the coverage is a count of the kinds that the tools read against the kinds that the estate has, ordered by cost that code computed ([rule 25](../../AGENTS.md)).
- **The page "What the AI looked at"** shows a block "The parts that the AI looked at" with a row for each beat, a column that names the beat in the table of findings and the new end word, "Its tokens ran out".

## One line for each tool call

**Why.** The first real run of one open session (2026-11-17, read by the orchestrator in Application Insights) made 22 calls in 450 seconds and 378,575 tokens, and found one thing that it had found before. The model had been called 24 times, about 3.5 seconds each, so about 85 seconds of the 450 were the model's: the rest, about 360 seconds, were tools, about 15 seconds for a call. Nothing in the History said which tool, for what, or how long: the private record of the investigation says what it looked at and not how long each read took, and the people who read a real run cannot read it. So each tool call that runs writes one line, `investigation.call`, and the run can be read from the History alone.

**What a line holds**, all words and numbers that code checks: `beat` and `n` (the call in its beat, the reads that code makes first), `by` (`code` for the reads that code makes for a beat, `agent` for the calls of the Investigator), `tool` (a tool of the toolbox, `preload`, or `unknown` for any other name), `resourceType` (only when the arguments hold a resource type in the shape that Azure gives, as `type_word` of the toolbox checks it, and never any other text of the arguments: it is not called `type`, which is the kind of the line), `handles` and `metrics` (how many it asked about, not which), `ok`, `error` (when it did not work: the word of the toolbox's closed list, or `budget-used-up`; any other word is `other`), `chars` (of the answer) and `seconds` (by the toolbox's own clock, to a tenth) and, only when the picture of a `profile` was kept and answered again with no read, `savedSeconds` (what that read took the first time). No handle, name, id, tag, text of an agent, text of an answer or amount is in it, and a test feeds it hostile text to show that. The first eight calls that the Investigator asked for after a beat's budget was used up, and that were refused, have a line too (`ok` false, `error` `budget-used-up`, no seconds): what it still wanted is what a person reading the History looks for; the others are counted by the line of the beat.

**How many.** For each beat at most its calls (3 by default, 16 at the ceiling), the 9 reads that code makes (five listings, the two pictures and the list prices of one meter, which are two reads when the product shows no price or only the line of the meter itself, or the price list could not be read) and the read ahead, and 8 refused: 168 lines for eight beats by default and 272 at the ceilings; one open session has at most 40 and 8 refused. A line is about 290 characters (about a kilobyte in Application Insights with its own fields, an estimate), so a run adds about 170 KB by default and 270 KB at the ceilings: a few hundred kilobytes a run is small against what the rest of the app writes, though it was not priced here, and no field is cut. `host.json` has the sampling of Application Insights off, so no line is dropped on the way. The check of the Hub counts them like the others (`investigationLines` of the end of the run) and waits for them, which is more lines to wait for and nothing else.

**What the Hub prints.** `tools/cases-check.py` prints, in the log of a public repository too, a line for each beat (kinds, what code read first, kinds looked at, calls, tokens, seconds, how it ended, findings) and, from the call lines, how many tool calls ran and how long, how many gave no answer and why, the reads of code against the calls of the Investigator, and the calls and the seconds of each tool, the longest first. Counts, seconds and tool names: a name that is not a plain lower-case word is `other`, and the resource type is never printed. One more line adds up, over all the findings, the counts that each `investigation.finding` carries of why a claim was not confirmed and of the resources that were too small to open a case (below): counts of the code's own reasons, no handle and no text.

**What it cannot tell.** Which metric, how many days, which grain, the page of a listing or what an answer said are not in a line: the line says which tool took the time, for how many resources, and whether it worked. The model's own time is a beat's seconds less its calls' seconds.

## What runs at the same time, and what does not

**Inside a call, as before:** the subscriptions of one read (4 at a time, the limit of Cost Management), the parts of a picture (`profile`: up to 6 reads, in 60 seconds) and the metrics (8). **New, for the reads that code makes for a beat:** `Toolbox.preload` reads the resources of the beat's kinds at the same time, one question of Resource Graph for each, in a pool of at most five (the quota of Resource Graph is the identity's, so the pool is no wider than the five kinds of a seed). The workers only read; what they read is registered by the thread that called, in the order of the kinds, by the code that registers a listing's own read, so no state of the toolbox is touched by two threads; and the method holds the toolbox's lock as a call does, so nothing runs beside it. The listings that follow find the resources in the caches and ask nothing. A type that is read already, a word that has not the shape of a type and a single kind are left alone, and a read that fails is left to the listing, which asks again and says why in its own words. A test shows that the reads overlap (a fake whose questions wait until all are in flight, so that reads one after the other would not pass), that the answers and the handles are what the listings give alone, and that the lock holds. The History has one line for it (`by` `code`, `tool` `preload`).

**What it is worth is an estimate.** After the first, a listing of a kind is one question of Resource Graph, about a second or two; five in a row are 5 to 10 seconds and together about 2: under 10 seconds a beat, about 60 of the 1,440 seconds of a Watch of eight beats. The reads that take the time are the pictures (24 seconds at the day and 39.5 at the hour for five resources on the real estate, the measure above) and the calls of the agent, and a picture is parallel inside its call already and needs the handles that the listings give, so it cannot start before them. The time of the pair was estimated and not timed there; the seeds of the six beats were then timed on the real estate (250 seconds, above), and in a real run the lines above will show the time tool by tool.

**Not built: the pictures of the next beat prepared while the model of this beat works.** It is not safe on this toolbox, and what it could win is small:

1. The Foundry client and the code's own reads share one HTTP client (`run.py` builds `Foundry(..., client, ...)` and `Arm(client, ...)`). During a model call the Foundry client replaces the client's transport by a wrapper that keeps the last answers (for the words of its own errors) and counts the answers that say "not now", and it sets the client's deadline to the end of the beat. A read from a second thread would go through that wrapper, so that its answers would be taken for the model's and its limits counted as the service's, and under the deadline of the beat. [Proposal 12](12-investigation-toolbox.md) reached the same answer for a read ahead.
2. `Toolbox.call` holds one lock for a whole call, by design: the time of a call, the handles, the evidence and the caches are one state. A read ahead through the toolbox would be serialized with the agent's own calls: it could overlap only the model's thinking (about 3.5 seconds a step by the measure above, so under half a minute for a beat) and the agent's next call would wait for it.
3. A beat counts its calls, its log, its allowance (`extra_calls`) and the kinds that it looked at by position in one log. A read ahead in the middle of another beat would put its calls in the wrong beat or need locks round all of it, and the order of the log, and so the numbers of the handles and the lines of the History, would depend on timing.

What would make it safe is a second `Arm` with an HTTP client of its own for the read ahead and a toolbox whose caches take their own lock: a change of the toolbox that is larger than this record and is worth making only if the lines show that the seeds are what ends the beats. The gain is at most the model's thinking time of a beat, an estimate.

## What this does not do

- It does not prove that beats find more than one session did. The first real run is that test, and it can be scored by the lines above: each beat's calls, tokens and seconds, how it ended, how many of its kinds its tools read, and what each concluded.
- It does not prove the numbers of tokens, seconds and dollars: they are one measured session, the sizes of the code's own texts and the fakes, with four characters a token and two prices for the gateway. The seeds were timed on the real estate by the orchestrator, before the picture time of 25 seconds and the allowance of 100 seconds for the model existed: what those two change is by construction and tested on the fakes (the seconds that a picture of code is given, and a model time that does not follow the reads), and only a real run shows the seconds. The number of metrics that a picture reads at the hour is bounded by a count as well (20 of the resource's metrics, the greatest daily maximum first, after the day is read), by a plain constant that names no kind; the time of a picture is bounded too.
- It does not widen what Meternaut may change: nothing here touches the runner, the approval, the delegated sign-in or a family of change, and the guards, the caps and the limit of open requests are not widened.
- A kind that the bill names and no resource has (cost lines of a deleted resource) is a kind with a count of none, and the beat says so. A resource that sits behind an attachment is found by the links of the dearest resource, if the agent leaves its part for it; nothing makes it.
- It does not prove the time that the read ahead saves, nor that the lines of the tool calls are enough to read a real run by: both are what the first real run shows. The lines are tested against fakes whose calls are scripted, and the number of lines in the telemetry and the time that the check of the Hub waits for them were not measured.
- What the Hub must run: `cases` (the template carries the floor of a case, `caseMinUsd`, and the seconds of the investigation, 1,440, which are on `main` already; the package carries `host.json` and the code, and with it the eight beats of two kinds), then `cases-run` once, which waits 38 minutes and whose container group is given 45 (both are on `main` already, so no change of the workflow comes with this record). No new right and no new resource; one new setting of the template, the floor of a case. The worst Watch is one minute over the 38 that the check waits (see the arithmetic of the seconds): the owner's workflow, not changed here.
